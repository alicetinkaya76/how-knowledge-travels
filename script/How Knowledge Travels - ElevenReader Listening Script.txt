How Knowledge Travels

As-salamu alaykum wa rahmatullah. I’m Ali Cetinkaya. Welcome. We come together as Muslims with different languages, cultures and journeys into the faith. Find a coastline you recognize on this map. Which way round were you expecting the world to be?

In the manuscript’s orientation, south is at the top. Africa sits above the Mediterranean, and Europe below it. Turning the page makes familiar places recognizable. The map exposes a habit in its readers.

The geography behind this later copy was completed in one thousand one hundred and fifty-four by al-Idrisi, a Muslim scholar working for Roger the Second, the Christian king of Sicily. Their partnership places Islamic learning inside a world of encounters. A scholar’s work could serve people beyond his own religious community and enter a different setting.

By Islamic civilization, I mean societies shaped by Islam, where Muslims, Christians, Jews and others worked in many languages and professions. Understanding that world means asking how people learned from one another while bringing their own purposes to the work.

Our journey asks what people had to change before unfamiliar knowledge could become understandable and useful.

Some of those encounters have already reached us through familiar, ordinary English words.


You already speak this history

In my project Rihlat al-Kalimat, the journey of words, I follow words between languages. Zero, algebra and algorithm are three whose journeys reach into our everyday lives. They name ways of calculating that people learned well enough to teach to others.

One carries a person’s name. Choose which one, silently or in the chat.

Algorithm carries the name al-Khwarizmi. Algebra comes from al-jabr, in the title of his book. Zero comes through a longer journey from sifr. Later, we will hear Fibonacci describe Indian numerals learned in North Africa and the Arabic name attached to their zero.

A pupil learns a useful calculation; later pupils may use it without knowing its earlier language. The word preserves a clue to that connection.

We will follow the clue back to the work: what did these methods enable someone to do, and how were they made teachable?

That means approaching the past with attention, an invitation we also encounter in the Qur’an.


Travel, and look

Fa-siru fi l-ardi fa-nzuru. So travel through the land and look.

In Surah Ali Imran, verse one hundred and thirty-seven, the invitation concerns earlier peoples who denied the message and what became of them. Looking at the past calls for reflection on its consequences.

That invitation gives our journey a purpose beyond admiring beautiful objects. A medical page raises a question about who could understand its words. A damaged book makes us ask what its readers had lost. An instrument with added writing reveals people trying to use it in a new setting.

As Muslims, we can take an interest in this inheritance while examining how it actually worked. Careful attention lets us recognize both an achievement and the labour it required.

That care belongs to amanah: knowledge received as a trust.


Knowledge is a trust

Receive. Test. Add. Pass on. These four verbs help us follow what happens to that trust.

Receiving gives someone access to earlier work. Testing carefully checks the evidence and the reasoning before someone relies on a claim. Adding supplies a correction or an explanation. Passing on makes that work available for someone else to learn.

Rabbi zidni ilma. My Lord, increase me in knowledge. The prayer leaves us open to learning, including discovering an error in something we inherited or produced ourselves.

In Baghdad, that responsibility began with a practical problem: how could a physician study a medical book written in a language they could not read?


A medical book finds Arabic readers

A physician may know that an older author discusses a treatment, yet have no usable access to the account. The book may be in Greek, while the physician studies in Arabic. Someone must understand both the language and the medicine well enough to make the account available. That need helps explain the work of translators in ninth-century Baghdad.

This Arabic page shows what a long engagement with Greek medicine could produce. Made in one thousand two hundred and twenty-four, it belongs to a later illustrated copy of Dioscorides. The Met calls it Physician Preparing an Elixir and places it in Iraq or northern Jazira, possibly Baghdad. Look at the work with vessels beneath the Arabic text: a learned medical tradition has a place in an Arabic book.

The earlier Greek manuscript, now in Vienna, brings together the author, a painter and a plant. These different scenes show the tradition being presented to readers centuries apart. The Arabic volume gave readers another language in which to study it, illustrate it and continue making copies.

Translation therefore changed who could participate. A learner reading Arabic could enter this discussion without first becoming a reader of Greek. A teacher could explain the work in the language of that learner’s study. A shared language also allowed readers to bring together explanations previously encountered through different linguistic traditions. Producing such access demanded specialist judgement: an elegant sentence would be little help if its medical meaning had been misunderstood.

The records name Istifan ibn Basil as a translator of Dioscorides and Hunayn ibn Ishaq as a reviser. Both belonged to Christian communities. Syriac, used by many Christians in the region, was also a language of medical learning and translation. Muslim patrons and practitioners belonged to the Arabic medical culture in which this expertise mattered.

We are seeing how Islamic civilization could draw on skills held in different communities. Arabic became a meeting place for work that had several languages and religious histories behind it. But even an able translator could only examine the manuscripts available.

What happened when a book worth translating could not be found complete? Hunayn tells us about that search himself.


Hunayn searches for a missing argument

Before Hunayn could translate a book, someone had to find a copy. A title might be famous while its actual pages were scattered or lost. His letter about translations of Galen describes that problem from inside the work. The later manuscript of his treatise on the eye shown here introduces the physician and author whose account we are following.

He wanted Galen’s On Demonstration, a work on proof: how an argument establishes a conclusion. That made completeness especially consequential. An argument depends on connections between its parts. If a step disappears, a reader may retain the conclusion while losing the reason for accepting it.

Another scholar had already searched hard for a complete Greek manuscript. Hunayn searched too.

He names northern Mesopotamia, Syria, Palestine and Egypt, as far as Alexandria. In Damascus he found about half the work, incomplete and out of sequence. The distance on the map represents an effort to recover the connections within a book. Reaching another city mattered if a copy there could supply what the previous one lacked.

Think about reading a proof with its opening missing. You may be able to translate every surviving word and still be unable to explain why the conclusion follows. Or a section may refer back to something you cannot locate. Better language skills alone cannot supply an absent argument.

Hunayn describes his reluctance to translate before reading the whole work. He also records translating portions into Syriac and identifies parts and gaps. He made recovered material available while giving readers a way to recognize what remained missing.

That shaped the inheritance available to later students. Students inherited what could be recovered. A missing argument also meant losing a model for constructing arguments of their own. Translation widened access without undoing every earlier loss.

By recording the gaps, he also told later searchers what still needed to be found.

Finding more manuscripts could also change a translation already in use. In another book by Galen, Hunayn returned to work he had done twenty years earlier.


Hunayn returns to his own translation

Hunayn had been about twenty, or a little older, when he translated Galen’s On Sects into Syriac for a physician. This introduction to different schools of medical thought reached him through one Greek manuscript. He later described that copy as very faulty. Whatever its defects, it was the source available for his first translation.

At about forty, his pupil Hubaysh asked him to correct the Syriac. By then, Hunayn had several Greek copies and more experience. A translation already used by another person could now be checked against evidence unavailable when he made it.

Where should he begin: with the Greek copies or the Syriac wording? Think about which could tell him what had gone wrong.

He began with the Greek. He compared the manuscripts and established a revised text. Then he checked the Syriac against it and corrected his translation. This sequence addressed two separate sources of error: what the manuscript said, and how he had understood and expressed it.

If a clause has dropped out of your only copy, knowing Greek perfectly will not bring it back. You can faithfully translate an incomplete sentence. Another manuscript may preserve the missing connection. Once you recover it, you may need to change the translation too, because the author’s argument now means something different.

The repair helped readers distinguish a damaged sentence from a difficult medical idea. Those problems require different responses. A better source gave them a stronger basis for asking whether Galen’s account was convincing.

The photograph shows another Galen work, On Simple Drugs, surviving in Syriac beneath later writing. It makes this intermediate language visible. In the case of On Sects, Hunayn’s letter lets us follow what happened next: a few years after the revision, he translated the Syriac into Arabic for Muhammad ibn Musa.

That Arabic version therefore carried the benefit of earlier comparison. Its readers did not have to repeat his search before they could begin studying the work. A Greek author, several manuscripts, a Christian translator, a pupil’s request and a patron’s support had all contributed to what they could now read.

Preparing an older text for another language also improved its reliability. Islamic medical learning grew through such carefully prepared versions, available for teaching and examination.

Hunayn also made his own earlier work subject to correction. By describing his sources and revisions, he let others judge what had improved. Trust in the translator could rest on an explanation of his work, as well as his reputation.

Yet a revised text sitting on one desk reaches few readers. How could it become a book someone else could obtain?


A translation needs a book

A translation could be intellectually ready and still unavailable to a student. Producing another copy required a writing surface, ink and skilled labour. These Qur’an folios, copied centuries apart, show two materials used for books: parchment made from animal skin, and paper made from fibres.

Supply mattered because every additional manuscript needed more material and more work. The scribe’s pen and prepared page turned words into a legible copy. Binding then made those pages into something a reader could repeatedly consult.

Folded sheets form gatherings; sewing holds them together, and a cover protects them. Keeping the leaves together also keeps an explanation in sequence. A student could return to an earlier page while studying a later one. The bound book had an ancient history, and Islamic workshops adapted its materials and construction.

Qalam carries Greek kalamos, a reed; ream carries Arabic rizma, a bundle. The vocabulary points towards the crafts and supplies on which learned life depended.

But how would a reader discover which book to request?


The bookseller’s map of knowledge

Ibn al-Nadim worked as a warraq, a trade connecting books, paper and copying. Around nine hundred and eighty-seven or nine hundred and eighty-eight he compiled the Fihrist, describing works across many subjects. Knowing a title could help a reader ask after a copy beyond their own collection.

Here we open his discussion of paper, preserved in an edition printed in one thousand eight hundred and seventy-one. Jafari and Talhi are among the trade names he records. A bookseller’s knowledge extended to what a book was made from.

Al-waraq al-sini: Chinese paper. This sentence connects papermaking in Khurasan with Chinese production. The names in this paragraph make the wider supply network visible within an account of books.

The two kinds of knowledge belong together. A reader could learn that a useful work existed while still lacking a copy to study. Materials and skilled labour turned that discovery into access. Learned life depended on people who could connect a request for a text with the means of producing it. The warraq stood where readers, texts and supplies met.

That Chinese connection now takes us beyond Baghdad to follow the material itself.


Paper travelled through a network

Paper affected who could get hold of an argument and how often it could be copied. That gives this map its place in our story: the work of scholars depended on a material supply that had to keep reaching their communities.

Manufacture began in China. By around eight hundred, paper was being made in Baghdad. The movement involved both finished sheets and the skills needed to establish workshops. Copying could spread a text; learning the craft could sustain a local supply.

Fibres, water and trained hands mattered in each new setting. A line across the map compresses those practical requirements. Its date identifies a surviving witness or a record of manufacture, rather than a single moment when an entire society acquired the craft.

Kashmir’s photograph records craft continuing in the east. The return arrow shows European paper reaching the region where a Qur’an was copied. Materials circulated in several directions, supporting work shaped by different communities’ needs.

A supply of books created opportunities for sustained study. What could an author help those readers understand? Al-Khwarizmi’s algebra will let us experience the difference that an explanation makes.


Algebra: turn an instruction into a reason

Books and materials made sustained study possible. Al-Khwarizmi used that opportunity to explain how a calculation works. Writing in ninth-century Baghdad, he gave rules and then supplied geometrical reasons. A learner could follow an instruction, but could also understand why it should produce the answer.

He presents his algebra as useful for inheritance, trade and measuring land. These are different practical situations, yet a calculation in each could have the same underlying form. Once learners recognized that form, they could apply an understood method to another problem.

His example says that a square and ten roots make thirty-nine. Root here means the unknown side of the square. In modern symbols, x squared plus ten x equals thirty-nine. His exposition uses words; our moving pieces make the reasoning visible.

We have the area of the unknown square, plus ten copies of its side. All together, they make thirty-nine. How can we arrange those areas so that the unknown side becomes easier to find?

We split ten x into two strips, each five by x. One goes beside the square and one below it. Nothing has been added, so their total area remains thirty-nine. But the arrangement has revealed a useful shape: nearly a larger square, with one corner missing.

That corner is five across and five down. Work out its area, and think about what must happen to the other side of the equality if we fill it.

Five times five gives twenty-five. We add twenty-five to the picture and to thirty-nine, making sixty-four. We now have a complete square whose side is eight. That side includes the added strip of width five, so the original unknown side is three.

Three squared is nine; ten threes make thirty. Together, thirty-nine. The answer works in the original problem.

Now listen to the rule behind those movements: halve ten, square the result, add it to thirty-nine, take the square root, then subtract five. Without the diagram, that might sound like a sequence to memorize. We can now explain each step. Halving gives the widths of the strips. Squaring supplies the corner. The square root gives the larger side, from which we recover the original one.

Understanding the reason also helps us detect an error. Adding the corner without adding twenty-five to thirty-nine would change the problem. Keeping the equality intact is what makes the conclusion trustworthy. A teacher can show that connection, and another learner can examine it.

The drawing also helps us move beyond this answer. Knowing three solves this case; understanding where the five and twenty-five come from lets us follow the procedure when the numbers change. That makes the explanation useful to a teacher whose next pupil brings another inheritance or land problem. Learning the reason includes learning how the steps fit the kind of problem being solved.

The later Arabic copy preserves diagrams with the argument. The Latin arithmetic witness concerns a different work associated with al-Khwarizmi. His algebraic title gives us algebra; his name travelling through arithmetic gives us algorithm. Written explanations and diagrams made these methods available for further teaching and development.

What would happen when mathematical learning reached people who needed it for the daily work of Mediterranean trade?


From Bejaia to a merchant’s classroom

Fibonacci tells us that his father, a Pisan official working with merchants in Bejaia, brought him there as a boy to learn calculation. In this port in present-day Algeria, a trading connection became an education. He encountered Indian numerals in the Arabic-speaking Mediterranean.

His Liber abbaci brought that learning to Latin readers. First written in one thousand two hundred and two and revised in one thousand two hundred and twenty-eight, it explains methods he had studied, examined and arranged for learning. This journey matters because a practice useful in one community could become the subject of instruction in another. That required someone to understand it and explain it for a different audience.

Look at the numerals. In twenty-five, the two means two tens. In two hundred and five, it means two hundreds. Zero holds the empty tens place, keeping two hundred and five distinct from twenty-five. Fibonacci calls it zephirum, a name connected with Arabic sifr.

Position keeps units, tens and hundreds in their places during calculation. The written steps let another person check the answer. In trade, an error can change a price or someone’s share; learning only the numeral shapes would miss what makes the system useful.

Over later centuries, Italian abbaco teachers taught merchants and artisans in local languages. Prices, currencies, measures and shared profits gave the lessons their purpose. Pupils practised decisions they would need to make, rather than learning numerals in isolation. Mathematical knowledge became usable through the language and examples of their working lives.

In our modern cloth example, three lengths cost twelve florins. Divide twelve by three: four florins per length. Five lengths therefore cost twenty. The quantity changes while the rate stays the same; buyer and seller can check the price. Beside it, the Treviso Arithmetic of one thousand four hundred and seventy-eight shows commercial instruction reaching print. Fibonacci’s Latin exposition, local classrooms and printed arithmetic represent different ways of making calculation learnable. Arabic-speaking North Africa belongs inside this history of European mathematical education as a place where learning happened.

A calculation can be checked through its reasoning. How do we examine an explanation of something we can actually see?


How can light turn a scene upside down?

The bed is upright and the air conditioner is where it belongs. Yet the building projected beside them has its roof below its windows, and outdoor treetops point towards the floor. Why should a small opening turn the world upside down? Solving that puzzle will give us a way to follow light from particular points outside to particular points on a wall.

Most of the daylight has been shut out of this bedroom. Light enters through a small opening and reaches the opposite wall. Each point outside sends light in many directions; the opening admits only a narrow selection. That restriction is useful. If light entered across the whole window, light from many outdoor points would spread over the same areas of wall. The small opening keeps enough of their positions separate for a scene to become recognisable.

Brown University made a larger room projection in two thousand twenty-three, using a lens to brighten the image. Follow the blue sky below the building and the inverted outdoor trees at the right. Those details settle which way up the image is, even when the building's repeated vertical lines make the reversal difficult to notice.

In eleventh-century Cairo, Ibn al-Haytham investigated light using dark spaces and openings. His expression al-bayt al-muzlim means dark room, as do the Latin words camera obscura. These modern photographs let us see a physical effect; his writing lets us ask how an investigator made such effects explainable. This is one way Islamic scientific work went beyond receiving older books: a question could be brought out of the text and examined in a deliberately arranged space.

Follow light from the top of the object. Its path through the opening carries it to the bottom of the screen. Light from the bottom reaches the top. The inversion follows from the crossing of straight paths. Move the screen farther away and those paths spread farther apart, enlarging the image in our model. We can predict a change before making it.

You could now work backwards from the image. A patch of light low on the wall must have come from a high point outside. The room lets us infer the direction of a source that we cannot see directly from where we stand.

The wall makes the transport of light visible. That is a more manageable problem than explaining everything involved in human sight. First establish where light goes; then ask how an eye receives it. One uncertainty remains even in the empty room, however.

When several light paths cross at the same opening, do they continue independently? Ibn al-Haytham's lamps let us investigate that precise question.


Three lamps, one opening

All three lamps send light through one opening. If their paths meet there, what happens afterwards? Ibn al-Haytham describes several lamps outside a dark chamber, facing a wall inside. Our plan view shows three, with colours and letters to make the paths easy to follow.

Each lamp produces a separate spot, in the reverse order. Before I screen lamp A, decide which spot should disappear. Notice that I will screen the lamp itself; the shared opening will remain clear.

Spot three disappears, while the other two stay. Unscreen A and its spot returns. Screening a different lamp removes the corresponding spot. Light from a particular source still reaches its own destination after meeting the other paths at the opening.

That is the point of this arrangement. Covering the opening would make every spot disappear and tell us little about their independence. Screening one source lets the other sources continue through the same opening. Their remaining spots show that crossing has not turned the light into an inseparable mixture. We can trace each source through the crossing to the wall. We have evidence about the crossing itself.

This result supports the geometrical explanation in Ibn al-Haytham's Optics. We can draw several light paths through one opening and follow each onward without losing its source. The separate lines in our diagram have an experimental reason behind them.

We have followed light onto a wall. To explain sight, we need the harder next step: where does incoming light arrive inside the eye?


A new answer inside the eye

Knowing where light goes in a room does not yet tell us how we see. Inside the eye, which structure receives it, and how could light from many points produce an ordered picture? The three pages on this slide show people working on that problem with answers that changed.

Ibn al-Haytham's Arabic manuscript shows two eyes and the nerves leading away from them. He argued that light enters the eye from objects. An explanation of sight therefore had to connect incoming light with the body's receiving structures. In his account, the crystalline humour towards the front of the eye played a central role in sensation. The chamber established a path through an opening. Identifying the receiving part of a living eye was a further problem.

The Book of Optics became available in Latin as De aspectibus. Roger Bacon studied it in the thirteenth century. His Perspectiva manuscript places geometrical diagrams alongside the discussion of sight. Latin readers now had an Arabic author's questions and arguments to work with. To use them, they needed to understand the diagrams, relate them to the eye, and decide which explanations they could defend. Translation made that further work possible. The discussion could proceed around shared diagrams: which lines connect which object points with which receiving structures?

Kepler's one thousand six hundred and four title page names Witelo, another author in this optical tradition. Kepler explained how refraction in the eye forms an inverted picture on the retina at the back. In the small schematic, light from the object's top reaches the picture's bottom. The receiving surface has changed from the one central to Ibn al-Haytham's account. A later investigator could use inherited optical reasoning while revising an important part of the explanation.

Even a retinal picture leaves a question unanswered: how does a person perceive what they see? Explaining image formation resolves an optical problem; it does not by itself resolve perception. We can value the Arabic contribution more clearly by seeing both its explanatory power and the further problems it helped make available for study. These pages preserve intellectual work that others could enter, examine and change.

Explaining a physical effect is one task. Designing a reliable sequence of effects is another. We return to one thousand two hundred and six, when al-Jazari needed a clock to announce an interval and prepare to do it again.


Design a cycle that begins again

A clock that announces an interval once has solved only half its problem. What gets it ready to announce the next one? Look inside al-Jazari's elephant and the main engineering question becomes clear: the end of one cycle must prepare the beginning of another.

Al-Jazari completed his book of ingenious devices in one thousand two hundred and six while serving the Artuqid court. The illustrated manuscript here was copied in one thousand three hundred and fifteen. Its elaborate exterior attracts attention, but the mechanism is what makes the design repeatable.

Inside the elephant, a perforated bowl floats in water. Water enters slowly until the bowl sinks. Its movement pulls a line, helping to release a ball above. The ball's weight turns a dragon-shaped mechanism and sets further actions in motion. Figures and sounds announce the interval. The linked mechanism also lifts and empties the bowl so that it can float and fill again.

Our cutaway follows fill, release and reset. Half an hour is the intended interval. The twenty and forty-five minutes shown for different openings are illustrative comparisons. Faster filling means an earlier release; the model helps us connect a physical adjustment with its consequence.

Suppose the bowl rises but fails to empty. The elephant may have announced the first interval beautifully, yet it cannot repeat the same timing. Or suppose a line catches: water can keep entering without releasing the next ball. We can now locate a failure within the sequence instead of treating the whole machine as a mystery. Checking one successful performance would miss that failure; the maker has to follow the mechanism into its next cycle.

That is what an engineering book can pass on. A later maker needs the relationships between parts, including the return movement that a spectator might never notice. Al-Jazari's work belongs in this history because courtly craftsmanship became a written design that another person could study and attempt to reproduce.

Skilled work takes time, materials and support. How did Islamic societies provide those conditions for people who taught and learned?


Who pays? Who teaches?

Al-Jazari needed time, materials and support to explain his clock. Learning from a book also costs something: a teacher's time, a place to sit, and a copy within reach. The objects here show how a community could meet those needs and decide whose teaching deserved trust.

This Qur’an stand was made in one thousand three hundred and sixty for endowment to a madrasa. Its inscription records a gift for learning. Waqf means an endowment. Income assigned from a shop or estate could support teachers, buildings or books. That arrangement gave a gift a continuing effect. Instead of paying for one lesson, revenue could keep making lessons possible after the donor's lifetime. The stand belongs to that larger effort to make access repeatable. That could make the difference between a book being present in a city and a learner having the time and guidance to study it.

Access alone could not establish someone's competence. This Ottoman calligraphy diploma, written by Ali Ra'if Efendi in one thousand seven hundred and ninety-one, authorized an approved learner to sign their work. Ijazah means authorization. Its meaning depended on the practice: permission to sign calligraphy recognized a different accomplishment from permission to transmit a particular book. In each case, a teacher's judgment connected a learner's claim with work someone had examined.

Karima al-Marwaziyya taught al-Bukhari’s hadith collection in eleventh-century Mecca. When a student copied seven pages from her text, she insisted on joining the comparison with the original. Her authority included checking the words that would circulate under her teaching.

In Damascus, reading records name Zaynab bint al-Kamal as the presiding authority, with scholars coming to receive hadith through her. These women enlarge our picture of who exercised authority in Islamic learning. Students depended on their expertise; the record of transmission depended on their names. Their participation shaped what others could responsibly teach.

Together, these examples explain two conditions for learning to continue: resources that keep study possible, and knowledgeable people who take responsibility for its quality.

Dedicated support could also sustain a whole institution. Cairo, Oxford and Cambridge show different answers to that practical need.


Who funds a place to learn?

Sultan Hasan's complex in fourteenth-century Cairo used a waqf to support teachers and students in four Sunni schools of law. Dedicated income made study and residence possible together.

Merton's statutes of one thousand two hundred and sixty-four provided for scholars supported by estates. College comes through French from Latin collegium, an association. At Cambridge, Lady Margaret Beaufort founded a divinity professorship in one thousand five hundred and two.

Its stipend paid for teaching to continue. Chair comes through French and Latin from Greek kathedra, a seat. Each institution turned property income into protected time for learning. An institution could plan beyond a single donor or teacher. This comparison identifies a shared problem; establishing how one institution influenced another requires further evidence. The word's ancestry and the institution's history are separate questions.

Support created teaching time. Ibn Sina's Canon shows how a book could give that teaching an order.


How the Canon became a textbook

Ibn Sina called his Arabic work al-Qanun fi al-tibb, : a body of medical principles. Latin readers knew it as Canon medicinae and its author as Avicenna. Here, canon means a rule or standard. The title announces a systematic account of medicine; its later authority grew through use, teaching and argument.

Written in the eleventh century, it reached Latin through Gerard of Cremona’s twelfth-century translation. Its opening calls medicine knowledge,; Latin says medicina est scientia. That knowledge concerns health and its loss, so that health can be preserved or restored.

Five books connect general principles, simple drugs, diseases of particular organs, conditions affecting the whole body, and compound remedies. A student can connect an illness with an account of the body and find the relevant treatment discussion. The arrangement makes a vast subject teachable.

Why was that a major contribution in the Latin West? Medical teachers already had Hippocrates, Galen and collections of shorter works. The Canon offered an extensive medical system, developed in Arabic, within one sustained framework. Teachers could move between principles, signs of illness and treatment, then ask why a particular decision followed. A Muslim scholar’s synthesis became part of how Latin-reading physicians learned to reason about medicine.

Bologna’s statutes make that influence concrete. They assigned selected parts of Book One in the first year and other parts later, alongside Galen and Hippocrates. This was knowledge shaping a course of study. In fifteenth-century Paris, Jacques Despars spent over twenty years commenting on it. Its authority meant sustained explanation and debate, as well as repeated reading.

Between one thousand five hundred and one thousand six hundred and seventy-four, European printers produced some sixty editions of all or part of the Canon, mostly for university medical training. These printings belong to that continuing readership. The book had become infrastructure for learning: successive teachers could start from a shared framework and debate how it should be understood.

But what could a student actually learn from it? Let us open two passages about recognizable medical problems.


What could a student learn from the Canon?

These are two problems actually discussed in Book One. They show how the book connects knowledge about the body with decisions about illness. The facsimile is a later English translation.

First, a fractured bone. Ibn Sina describes bringing the separated parts together, supporting them with bandages, and keeping the injured part still. The reader can connect the procedure with the problem: the bone has lost its continuity, and movement can disturb its reunion.

The same paragraph includes a dietary theory of healing very different from modern medicine. Reading it historically means understanding the reasoning, including where its assumptions differ from ours.

Now a diagnostic problem. Ibn Sina associates sandy material in cloudy urine with a stone. But elsewhere he warns that food, a dirty vessel, or leaving the sample standing can alter its appearance. Before interpreting a sign, the learner must ask what produced it.

A student thus meets more than a treatment name. The book provides a sequence of questions: what changed in the body, what can I observe, and how does that observation support a judgment?

Checking what a book teaches could also reveal a problem in its numbers. Our next example comes from astronomy.


When a translator checks the numbers

Ptolemy's Almagest reached Arabic readers as al-Majisti and Latin readers through Gerard of Cremona. Its tables saved astronomers long calculations. A copied error could therefore spread into every calculation that used the faulty entry.

An arc follows a circle's edge; a chord joins the same endpoints in a straight line. Astronomers used such relationships to calculate positions in their geometrical models of the sky. The table turns an angle into a length without constructing the geometry again each time.

The written procedure checks the table, rather like a spreadsheet formula checking a result. Repeating its steps can reveal a disagreement that faithful copying would preserve. Later astronomical calculations depend on those entries, so one unchecked value could affect many results.

Historian Stefan Zieme compared Greek, Arabic and Latin tables and repeated the historical calculations. Some tables in Gerard's Latin tradition appear recalculated to agree more closely with Ptolemy's written examples. Gerard or a collaborator may have done this; the calculator remains uncertain. Translation here included mathematical scrutiny, producing values checked against the procedure as the work entered another language.

An astrolabe put mathematical relationships into moving parts that a learner could use beneath the sky.


The sky in your hand

An astronomical table stores relationships as numbers. An astrolabe makes some of those relationships movable, allowing an observer to connect the sky overhead with a scale in the hand. This one was made in Toledo by Muhammad ibn al-Saffar in one thousand twenty-nine or one thousand thirty. Its projecting tips mark selected stars; the openwork map rotates over a fixed plate.

Why does it need that second layer? The same stars appear at different heights when we travel north or south. The celestial pole stands higher as we go north, so the stars' paths meet our horizon differently. A star map alone cannot tell us what lies above a particular observer's horizon. The plate supplies that local information.

Changing our model from Baghdad to Istanbul changes the local horizon and altitude curves. On an instrument, a plate for the new latitude can be put beneath the same rotating star map. Choosing the right plate is part of obtaining a trustworthy result. Keeping the wrong one would mean comparing a real observation with a horizon drawn for somewhere else.

For a time measurement, an observer can first measure a known star's height with the sighting arm. Moving its pointer to that altitude on the correct side of the front sets the model to match the observed sky. With the date's solar position, the instrument's other scales then give the time. The brass turns an observed angle into something calculable.

The Toledo maker has therefore given mathematical astronomy a form that someone can carry and operate. Using it elsewhere requires attention to both place and procedure.

Separating the layers will show how the instrument accommodates a new horizon while keeping its star map.


Take an astrolabe apart

The separated parts make the design easier to understand. In this Dresden photograph, the case holds the plates; a solid plate carries the local horizon, while the openwork rete carries the stars. Rete means net in Latin; the Arabic name ankabut means spider.

The central pin holds them together and lets the star map turn. The alidade, from Arabic al-idada, is the sighting arm, usually on the back. Each part has a job: measure an angle, supply a local horizon, or move the stars against it. A user travelling north needs another latitude plate, while the star map can stay.

The layers allow a local adjustment without rebuilding the whole instrument. Separating the moving sky from a fixed horizon makes that possible.

But what if the parts still work and a new owner cannot read their labels?


One instrument, new readers

This instrument could still work after its owner's language changed. Made in eleventh-century al-Andalus and now in Verona, it carries Arabic engraving alongside later Hebrew writing and Western numerals. The additions let us examine two different jobs: making an instrument readable and deciding whether its numbers are right.

The original Arabic inscription names Cordoba and gives its latitude as thirty-eight degrees and thirty minutes. We now know why that value belongs here. The plate represents a local horizon, so choosing it affects how an observation is interpreted.

Above the Arabic, historian Federica Gigante reads a faint Hebrew addition as apparently saying Cordoba. The clear letters beside the photograph reproduce her proposed reading. Imagine a user who understands the instrument but reads Hebrew more readily than Arabic. A familiar city label could help that person select the existing plate and put it to use. An expensive piece of workmanship could serve another community without being made again.

Then someone added thirty-six; thirty-six and a half also appears. Gigante interprets these Western numerals as attempted amendments to the latitude. They are erroneous corrections. The later additions make the numerical information less accurate, even though another addition may have made the city name more accessible. A wrong number could lead someone to select the plate for a latitude its engraved curves do not represent.

That combination is crucial. An object does not improve automatically as it passes from hand to hand. Translation can remove a real obstacle, while an attempted correction can create a new one. A confident new number still needs checking against observation or a reliable table.

Here an instrument made in an Islamic society became working material for later readers using different scripts. The scratched brass preserves their engagement, including a mistake. It shows us both the reach of that scientific craftsmanship and the responsibility that comes with adapting it.

Chaucer faced the language problem too. His answer was to design an English lesson around one particular learner and his instrument.


Chaucer teaches the astrolabe in English

Lowys has an astrolabe made for Oxford's latitude, wants to learn, and knows little Latin. Handing him a learned Latin book would leave the practical problem unsolved. Chaucer therefore writes in English, beginning with the instrument's parts before teaching operations such as finding the time.

His prologue names Greek, Arabic, Hebrew and Latin learning. English can teach these conclusions too. He chooses manageable operations and accepts repetition when it helps Lowys remember. The lesson's success depends on what this learner can understand and do. A measured star height can become a time reading, with the book guiding his movements.

Find Aldebaran on the later drawing. Its name comes from Arabic al-Dabaran, the follower. A learner can retain that inherited name while learning in English how to use its pointer. Language need not erase the history of a method to make it useful.

Chaucer pairs the instrument with instruction fitted to its user. The Oxford plate suits Lowys's place; the English exposition suits his language; the sequence of lessons helps him learn how to use both.

Several examples have carried us westwards, from Arabic into Latin and English. The next manuscript shows why that direction cannot explain the whole history.


Paper travels in both directions

This Qur’an was copied around one thousand three hundred and forty in Iraq or Iran on Italian paper. Earlier, we followed papermaking westwards through Islamic lands. Here paper travels eastwards, through trade, to carry the words of the Qur’an.

The manuscript's study identifies a cross watermark. It is not visible in this photograph; that finding comes from examination of the paper. Wire attached to a papermaking mould leaves a thinner area in the sheet. Held against light, that area reveals the design.

For us as Muslims, the manuscript brings a familiar sacred text together with a material history that may be less familiar. The mark belongs to the sheet's manufacture. The scribe used that sheet for Qur’anic writing. The object connects devotion with ordinary decisions about obtaining a suitable surface on which to write.

Papermaking travelled from China through Central Asia and Islamic lands into Europe. Italian workshops then exported paper around the Mediterranean. That return movement changes the shape of our story. A simple relay, with one civilization handing knowledge to the next and leaving the scene, cannot explain this sheet. Islamic communities remained buyers, users and makers within continuing exchanges. The same material acquired another purpose through the people who chose it.

West African libraries reveal another community making books serve its own teaching, worship and intellectual life.


Timbuktu’s living archive

Timbuktu grew where Saharan routes met the world of the Niger. Learned families copied, taught and composed works in law, devotion and history, alongside arithmetic and astronomy. These libraries preserve evidence of African Muslims deciding what was worth studying and how to explain it.

The astronomy manuscript, copied in one thousand seven hundred and thirty-three, belongs to the Mamma Haidara family library. Its diagram represents the rotation of the heavens. The book teaches, among other subjects, how stellar movements can identify the beginning of seasons. Recurring patterns in the sky become a way of organizing time. That practical question gives the diagram a purpose beyond decoration.

Al-Rasmuki’s arithmetic commentary explains addition, subtraction, multiplication and division through charts and worked problems. Hisab means calculation or reckoning. The Library of Congress identifies the work as a student text used in Timbuktu and North Africa. Like the merchant lessons we met earlier, it teaches operations that a learner can repeat and check.

This changes Timbuktu's place in our journey. West Africa belongs within the history of people explaining, calculating and composing. Its scholars had work of their own to do. Their books let us encounter that work through particular pages.

Keeping that evidence available required another kind of knowledge. During the crisis of two thousand twelve to thirteen, armed groups occupied Timbuktu and threatened its heritage. Malian owners and librarians moved collections away. Abdel Kader Haidara and SAVAMA-DCI helped organize the evacuation. Families entrusted fragile manuscripts to others because remaining in place also carried danger.

Metal chests travelled in stages over months. Some journeys used donkey carts or canoes before vehicles carried the collections onward. Local knowledge and relationships made movement possible: someone had to judge a route, arrange transport and earn a family's confidence. These custodians preserved the evidence through which the scholarship can still be studied.

The images we have examined come from a Library of Congress collaboration begun in December two thousand four, before the evacuation. HMML later digitized the Mamma Haidara collection between two thousand fifteen and two thousand twenty-three. These are distinct chapters of preservation.

A digital copy gives distant readers access; the original still needs care. Amanah an entrusted responsibility, names something concrete here. People accepted responsibility for pages that held their families' learning, so that this intellectual history could remain open to study.

In nearby Djenne, responsibility also takes a material form. A mosque survives through people teaching one another how to maintain it.


One prayer, two buildings

Djenne in Mali and Demak in Java share a purpose familiar to us: making a place for Muslim prayer. Their different forms show how that shared practice could become part of local life. Look at the skills each building requires.

At Djenne, sun-dried earth bricks carry mud plaster, and projecting timber provides access for maintenance. The present Great Mosque was rebuilt in one thousand nine hundred and seven within a much older earthen building tradition. Weather wears its surface, so survival depends on repeated repairs. A mason must know how to prepare the mixture, apply it and restore the protective layer after damage.

Working beside an experienced mason can teach what a description alone leaves difficult to grasp: how the plaster should feel and how it behaves against a wall. The building therefore preserves knowledge through the work needed to keep it standing. Maintenance also becomes an occasion for teaching.

At Demak, three roof levels draw the eye upwards. The mosque's architectural account describes thin wooden shingles, columns and four principal teak supports. A carpenter needs to understand how the supports carry the roof and how its parts meet. Javanese building traditions supplied a form through which the community could make a place of Muslim worship.

Our familiar practice helps us read these differences. Someone accustomed to another mosque may first notice the unfamiliar roof or wall. Knowing what those forms do reveals a community making room for the same prayer.

Their shared orientation also requires different directions locally. Modern initial bearings towards Mecca are about seventy-three degrees from Djenne and two hundred and ninety-four from Demak, clockwise from true north. These calculations illustrate geography; they do not establish either building's historical alignment.

Facing one qibla does not make the earth or the available timber identical. Islamic unity could be expressed through a shared act while local expertise determined how to shelter it. These buildings help us recognize cultural difference within a common religious life. Their materials are part of that history.

Writers made a related adaptation: Arabic-based letters could carry the languages through which their own communities understood the world.


Jawi and Ottoman Turkish

These pages use Arabic-based writing systems to write Malay and Ottoman Turkish. Recognizing the letters does not mean knowing the language. English and Turkish can both use Latin letters while remaining different languages; the same distinction applies here. A script provides written signs, while a language supplies words, sounds and grammar.

The two large letters reveal how adaptation works. Malay Pasai begins with P, a sound Arabic does not have. Standard Jawi represents it with a shape like fa' and three dots above. Ottoman Turkish uses a shape like ba' with three dots below, as in Piri. These small changes let writers represent a sound their readers needed.

The Malay manuscript was copied at Semarang on Java in one thousand seven hundred and ninety-seven. It tells the history of Pasai, a kingdom in northern Sumatra. A copy made on another island shows how a language could carry a community's past across distance. The reader could encounter that past through Malay words and sentences.

Its title includes hikayat, related to Arabic hikaya, a story or narrative. A borrowed word remains visible within a work whose language is Malay. The shared writing tradition could carry a local history in its readers’ own language.

Now look at Paros in a later copy of Piri Reis's Book of Navigation. Coastlines and compass roses make geographical relationships visible. The Ottoman Turkish writing belongs to the same task: connecting information on a page with places a sailor might encounter. The words name places and explain what the coastline alone cannot tell a sailor.

Adapted letters made a kingdom’s history and maritime knowledge expressible in different languages. For Muslims whose first language is different from Arabic, this relationship may feel familiar: a shared religious connection can coexist with several languages of understanding and expression.

These adaptations bring us back to our title. How did knowledge travel, and what happened as Islamic life took root in different cultures?


What made these journeys possible?

Our title joins two questions: how knowledge travels, and how Islam is lived in different cultures. The objects answer them together. A work could travel because someone made it understandable and useful within another community.

Arabic connected scholars across distances; translation opened books to further readers. Checking a manuscript or recalculating a table could expose an error. Diagrams explained a method, while adapted letters allowed a language to carry new kinds of writing. Endowments supported study; teachers and families sustained its human relationships. Algebra's diagram, the Canon's sequence and the Jawi letter connected inherited resources with concrete tasks: understanding an argument, arranging study, or expressing a sound.

Muslims, Christians and Jews participated in particular settings whose opportunities and constraints changed over time. Prayer gave some work a purpose; medicine, trade, government and curiosity supplied others. These connections explain a history of learning more fully than a list of celebrated names can.

There may be something recognizable here: familiar faith alongside unfamiliar words, or an explanation that makes a difficult idea accessible. Some of us embraced Islam recently; others have practised for many years. We share our faith while bringing different languages and histories to it.

The scholar in Mecca, the scribe on Java and the family in Timbuktu also lived that combination differently. Their places within this history help us recognize the breadth of an inheritance we already share. Their lives give substance to a shared belonging expressed in several languages and cultural experiences.

Knowledge travelled through people doing this work together.
