How Knowledge Travels. Islam in Different Cultures.

Dr. Ali Cetinkaya.

How Knowledge Travels

As-salamu alaykum wa rahmatullah. I’m Ali Cetinkaya. Welcome. We have come together as Muslims from different places, and we bring different journeys into the faith. Before we begin, find a coastline you recognize on this map. Which way round were you expecting the world to be?

In the manuscript’s orientation, south is at the top. Africa sits above the Mediterranean, and Europe below it. Turning the page makes familiar places easier for many of us to recognize, although the coastlines themselves have not moved. We have changed the habits with which we read them.

The geography behind this later copy was completed in one thousand one hundred and fifty-four by al-Idrisi, a Muslim scholar working for Roger the Second, the Christian king of Sicily. That partnership gives us our starting point: knowledge could take shape where people with different religious commitments shared a task.

When I say Islamic civilization this evening, I mean societies shaped by Islam, where Muslims, Christians, Jews and others worked in several languages and many professions. Their shared world included scholars and rulers, but also merchants, craftspeople, teachers and families. We will meet particular people making particular choices; religion is part of that history, alongside the conditions that made their work possible.

Our question is: what changes when knowledge finds a new community? Sometimes the language changes. Sometimes an explanation is tested, or an instrument is adapted to another place. The people receiving it have work of their own to do.

Traces of those encounters are already in the words we use.


You already speak this history

In my project Rihlat al-Kalimat, the journey of words, I follow what happens as words move between languages. Three on this screen will accompany us today: zero, algebra and algorithm. Even if Arabic is new to you, these may already be familiar companions.

One carries a person’s name. Choose which one, silently or in the chat.

Algorithm carries the name al-Khwarizmi, a scholar whose work we will try together. Algebra comes from al-jabr, part of the title of his book. Zero comes through a longer journey from sifr. We will meet a Latin author who explains both the Indian numerals he learned and the Arabic name for their zero.

These words are small pieces of evidence. An English word can preserve a trace of an Arabic title, while that Arabic work draws on learning from elsewhere. Names can acknowledge a debt even after the people using them have forgotten the journey. Following the word opens a door; the fuller story asks who learned the method, why it was useful, and what they did with it.

The invitation to travel also asks something of the traveller: pay attention to what you find.


Travel, and look

Fa-siru fi l-ardi fa-nzuru. So travel through the land and look.

In Surah Ali Imran, verse one hundred and thirty-seven, this invitation concerns the fate of earlier peoples who denied the message. Travel is joined to reflection on what happened before us. That context matters when we bring the verse into a conversation about history.

Our journey tonight will be through surviving pages, instruments and buildings. We can admire their beauty and still ask what they actually tell us. A date locates a copy; a note may identify a reader; a diagram can preserve an argument. Putting these clues together takes patience, especially when something has been lost. Looking also means being willing to revise our first impression.

For me, looking carefully belongs with caring about what we learn.

That care has an ethical name: amanah, a trust.


Knowledge is a trust

Knowledge is a trust. Receive it. Test it. Add to it. Pass it on. These four verbs are a way of examining our responsibility. In the stories ahead, several may belong to the same person, even to the same afternoon’s work.

Rabbi zidni ilma. My Lord, increase me in knowledge. This prayer leaves room for something we have yet to understand, including a correction to something we thought we knew. It gives humility a place in the work of learning. We will return to it at the end.

In Baghdad, receiving an older medical tradition began with a very practical obstacle: getting hold of words a physician could read and examine.


A medical book finds Arabic readers

A physician opening an old medical book wants more than an impressive author’s name. The words must make sense, the account must be usable, and there must be a way of checking what the author meant. Those needs helped create work for translators in ninth-century Baghdad.

The page before us is a later result of Arabic readers’ long engagement with Greek medicine. It was made in one thousand two hundred and twenty-four, several centuries after the translators we are about to meet. The Met calls it Physician Preparing an Elixir and cautiously places it in Iraq or northern Jazira, possibly Baghdad. Look at the seated figure working beside the vessels, with Arabic writing above and below him.

Here is a much earlier Greek manuscript of Dioscorides, now in Vienna. Its picture brings together the author, a painter and a plant. These are different scenes from different manuscripts. We are seeing two illustrated witnesses to the life of a medical tradition across centuries. They do not let us measure how one particular passage changed in translation.

Return to the Arabic page. To produce a book like this, several kinds of expertise had to meet. Someone needed access to a text, someone had to understand its language and medical vocabulary, and copyists and illustrators had to make a new volume. Readers and patrons gave that work a purpose.

The translation records let us name some of the people behind that process. Istifan ibn Basil translated Dioscorides, and Hunayn ibn Ishaq revised his work. Both belonged to Christian communities. Their work entered an Arabic medical culture sustained by patrons and practitioners, including Muslims. Syriac, a language used by many Christians of the region, was also an important language of medical study and translation.

So the scene is larger than a book moving from Greek into Arabic. Expertise could cross religious boundaries because people needed one another’s skills. Yet finding a person able to translate was only the beginning. The copy available to that translator might be damaged, incomplete, or simply absent.

Hunayn’s own account takes us into that difficulty: he went looking for a famous work and found scattered pieces.


Hunayn searches for a missing argument

The page on the screen comes from a later copy of Hunayn’s work on the eye. It belongs to the world of a physician who was also a teacher and author. For the search we are following, however, our evidence is his letter about translations of Galen. There he tells us which books he could find and what condition they were in.

One he wanted was On Demonstration, a work about proof. Galen was a medical authority, but this title concerned how an argument could establish its conclusion. Even a famous author’s work could be difficult to obtain. Hunayn says that another scholar had already searched hard for a complete Greek manuscript. He undertook a search himself.

Northern Mesopotamia, Syria, Palestine, Egypt, as far as Alexandria: that is the range he names. In Damascus he found about half the work. The parts were incomplete and did not run in sequence. Travelling farther had not put the whole argument into his hands.

Picture the reading problem, rather than the distance alone. A surviving section may refer to a proof in an earlier section that is missing. Another may begin after the step which would explain how the author got there. Knowing the language cannot restore those absent connections. A translator has to decide how much can responsibly be made available.

Hunayn describes his reluctance to translate before he had read the whole work. He also records translating portions into Syriac, identifying parts and gaps. His account allows us to see both the wish for completeness and the decision to work with what he could recover. He did not turn the search into a story in which persistence automatically brings back everything.

That result changes how we understand receiving knowledge. The inheritance was already uneven. Some works survived more fully than others; some readers had access to material that another reader lacked. The translator helped determine what the next community could study, while remaining dependent on copies he might never locate.

His list of what he found is useful precisely because it leaves the gaps visible. A later scholar can distinguish his recovery from the complete work he had hoped to read. That is a form of generosity too: another person receives the evidence needed to judge the limits of your achievement.

Missing pages were one obstacle. In a different book by Galen, Hunayn faced a harder personal task: returning to a translation he himself had made twenty years earlier.


Hunayn returns to his own translation

Hunayn had been about twenty, or a little older, when he translated On Sects into Syriac for a physician. This was an introduction to different schools of medical thought. He worked from a single Greek manuscript, which he later described as very faulty.

At about forty, he received a request from his pupil Hubaysh: correct the translation. By then several Greek copies had come into his possession. The request brought his younger work back to his desk, with more experience and better evidence available.

There is a real choice here. He could start polishing the Syriac wording, making difficult sentences clearer. Or he could return to the Greek and ask whether his first source had given him the right text to translate. Where would you begin: Greek or Syriac? Choose one, and think of a reason.

He began with the Greek manuscripts. He compared them and established a revised Greek text; then he checked the Syriac against it and corrected the translation. The order matters. A problem in a translation can begin before the translator has chosen a single word. If the manuscript leaves something out, a smooth translation may simply make the omission harder to notice.

Imagine a sentence whose last clause has dropped out in one copy but survives in another. This is an illustration of the problem, rather than a surviving example from this passage. Reading the second copy could change your understanding of the first. Only after that comparison would you be ready to judge whether your Syriac sentence expressed the argument. Checking the source and checking your interpretation are related jobs, but each needs attention.

The photograph beside the sequence shows a surviving Syriac translation of another Galen work, On Simple Drugs, recovered beneath later writing by special imaging. It gives us a glimpse of the material life of Syriac medicine. Our account of the revision of On Sects comes from Hunayn’s letter. Keeping those two pieces of evidence distinct lets each tell its own story.

A few years after revising the Syriac, Hunayn translated it into Arabic for Muhammad ibn Musa. Now an Arabic reader could encounter wording shaped by a Greek author, several Greek copies, a Christian translator, a pupil’s request and a patron’s support. Syriac was part of the work that made the Arabic version possible.

Notice where the movement has led. A new community gained access to a text, and the process of preparing that text also produced a more carefully examined version. Hunayn’s own earlier work became open to correction. His authority rested partly on being able to explain what he had checked.

We do not have to imagine him pleased or embarrassed when the request arrived. The choice he records is enough: he returned to the sources, revised what needed revising, and told others how he had done it. That is the kind of trust we can examine.

A corrected translation still had to become a book someone could obtain. The next part of this Baghdad story belongs to the people who made and supplied books.


A translation needs a book

The scholar’s revised words were only part of the cost of a manuscript. Someone also needed a writing surface, ink, time and trained hands. These two Qur’an folios, copied centuries apart, show parchment made from animal skin and paper made from fibres. A book trade depended on turning such materials into dependable supplies.

A scribe prepared a reed pen and set out the page. The penknife renewed the writing edge; the page needed room for text, headings and perhaps a later reader’s notes. Then separate leaves had to become a volume that would survive use.

Here the folded sheets form gatherings. Sewing joins them, and a cover protects the whole. The bound book had an ancient history; Islamic workshops adapted its materials and construction. Each stage required skill, and each contributed to what a reader could finally hold.

Qalam carries Greek kalamos, a reed; ream carries Arabic rizma, a bundle.

A reader also needed a way to discover which books existed and where to look for them. In tenth-century Baghdad, that was part of a bookseller’s world.


The bookseller’s map of knowledge

Ibn al-Nadim worked as a warraq, a trade connecting books, paper and copying. Around nine hundred and eighty-seven or nine hundred and eighty-eight he compiled the Fihrist, a remarkable account of books known to him in Baghdad. It includes religious writing, poetry, philosophy, mathematics and medicine. Finding out that a work existed could be the first step towards asking after a copy.

His catalogue also discusses the material carrying those works. The photographed page is from an edition printed in one thousand eight hundred and seventy-one, preserving his much earlier account.

Al-waraq al-sini: Chinese paper. In this passage he connects papermaking in Khurasan with Chinese production. Elsewhere in the paragraph, names associated with patrons and regions point to a varied paper trade. The vocabulary preserves connections that a sheet alone might not announce.

The bookseller’s knowledge and the papermaker’s skill met in the same economy. Manuscripts needed both someone to supply their material and someone who knew what readers were looking for.

That Chinese connection opens the map beyond Baghdad. Following paper shows how widely the material supporting these books travelled.


Paper travelled through a network

Hunayn’s improved translation depended on access to more copies. The supply of writing materials affected how many new copies could be made. Paper belongs inside this story of intellectual work.

Manufacture began in China, and paper travelled through Asian networks before Islamic rule. By around eight hundred it was being made in Baghdad, meeting demand from administration, scholarship and the book trade.

The dates identify evidence from different places. The lines connect them schematically. A merchant could carry sheets; establishing a workshop also required trained people, suitable fibres and water.

The network reaches European printing and an English mill, but it also extends east. Our Kashmir photograph records workers preparing a writing surface in nineteen seventeen. That dates the photograph, rather than the craft’s arrival.

Remember the return arrow: later, a Qur’an written on European paper will bring us back to it. We now have texts that can be found, compared and copied. What can scholars do with the knowledge they inherit?

In Baghdad, al-Khwarizmi showed a learner why a procedure worked. We will follow one of his arguments together.


Algebra: turn an instruction into a reason

Books and materials made sustained work possible. What could an author do with that opportunity? Al-Khwarizmi, writing in ninth-century Baghdad, faced a problem familiar to anyone who has taught a calculation: a learner can repeat the instruction without seeing why it works. His algebra gives rules and then supplies geometrical reasons.

He introduces the work as useful for practical matters, including inheritance, trade and measuring land. Different problems could share a mathematical form. Recognizing that form meant that each new case did not have to begin again from nothing. Let us stay with one example long enough to understand the argument.

His words describe a square and ten roots making thirty-nine. Here, root means the unknown side of the square. We show it in modern symbols as x squared plus ten x equals thirty-nine. His exposition uses words, so the symbols and moving pieces on this screen are our teaching model. The reasoning follows his geometric explanation.

The first area is x multiplied by x. Beside it we have ten copies of x. We want to find the side, but it is difficult to recognize the answer while the areas sit in this form. Try rearranging the ten copies without changing their total.

We split ten x into two strips, each five by x, and place one beside the square and one below it. The original area is still thirty-nine. The pieces almost make a larger square. Their shape now exposes a missing corner: it is five across and five down. Work out its area, and think about what we must do to the other side of the equality if we fill it.

The corner has area twenty-five. Adding it to the picture means adding twenty-five to thirty-nine as well. That gives sixty-four. We now know the area of a complete square, so its whole side is eight. But eight includes the strip of width five that we added. The unknown original side must be three.

Three squared is nine, and ten threes make thirty: together, thirty-nine. The check returns us to the original problem. We can also explain why the procedure worked. Halving ten gave the width of the strips; squaring five supplied the corner; taking the square root gave the larger side. Every instruction has acquired a place in the drawing. This is also why the corner matters so much. If we add it in the picture and forget to change thirty-nine, we have quietly solved a different problem. The drawing makes that mistake visible. Preserving equality is the thread that connects the rearrangement, the added corner and the final check. It lets someone else inspect our reasoning.

This later Arabic copy preserves diagrams alongside the written argument. The Latin arithmetic witness represents another work associated with al-Khwarizmi, concerning reckoning. One book’s title gives us algebra; the author’s name, travelling through Latin arithmetic, gives us algorithm. Those two word histories belong together without making the two books the same work.

The achievement here is a way of organizing and justifying mathematical procedures that others could study, teach and extend. We have experienced one small part of that work ourselves.

Another community needed mathematics for a different daily task: calculating in the busy trading towns of the Mediterranean.


From Bejaia to a merchant’s classroom

Fibonacci tells us that his father, a Pisan official working with merchants in Bejaia, brought him there as a boy to learn calculation. Bejaia is on the coast of present-day Algeria. A trading connection became an educational opportunity, and a young learner encountered the Indian numeral system in the Arabic-speaking Mediterranean.

In the prologue to his Liber abbaci, Fibonacci describes that education and his later study of different methods. He chose to explain Indian reckoning to Latin readers. The book first appeared in one thousand two hundred and two and was revised in one thousand two hundred and twenty-eight. He presents it as work he has examined, corrected and arranged to make learning easier. That should sound familiar after Hunayn’s return to an earlier translation.

Look at the numerals in this later manuscript. Their power depends on position. In twenty-five, the two stands for two tens. In two hundred and five, it stands for two hundreds; the zero holds the empty tens place. In Fibonacci’s Latin, its name appears as zephirum, connected with Arabic sifr. A new learner needed to acquire the meaning of the positions, as well as the shapes of the signs.

Over later centuries, Italian abbaco teachers addressed the needs of merchants and artisans in local languages. Their pupils practised prices, currency, measures and the division of profits. Learning meant repeated work on problems which resembled the decisions they would face in business. Fibonacci’s Latin book and these local classrooms belong to a broader history of mathematical teaching; one famous book alone cannot explain that whole development.

Our short cloth problem illustrates proportional reckoning. The printed book beside it, produced at Treviso in one thousand four hundred and seventy-eight, teaches commercial arithmetic. A new medium could now carry this practical instruction. The calculation on the screen is a modern example of that kind of lesson.

Here the new community changed the language, examples and setting of mathematical education. People learned inherited methods by putting them to work in their own circumstances.

A numerical rule can be made understandable through its reason. A claim about the visible world asks for another kind of evidence: what happens when we test it with light?


How can light turn a scene upside down?

Imagine a strangely decorated bedroom. The bed is where we expect it to be, and the air conditioner is still attached to the wall. But the building beyond the bed has turned upside down. Its roof lies below its windows, and the branches above it are the tops of outdoor trees pointing towards the floor. These are not pictures hung on the wall. They are light arriving from the scene outside.

The photograph records a modern pinhole demonstration. Most of the daylight has been shut out, leaving a small opening through which light reaches the wall. What makes this useful to us is the contrast between the ordinary room and the extraordinary position of the projected scene. We can separate the thing receiving the image from the thing represented in it.

Brown University created a much larger version in a studio in two thousand twenty-three. Here the repeated vertical lines of the building can fool us: they look quite similar either way up. Instead, follow the blue sky below the facade and the little outdoor trees projected at the right. Those details reveal the inversion. Brown’s team fitted a lens into a covered window to make the image brighter. It is a modern demonstration of a physical effect that we can investigate with a much smaller opening.

In eleventh-century Cairo, Ibn al-Haytham used dark spaces and openings to investigate how light behaves. His Arabic expression, al-bayt al-muzlim, means a dark room. The Latin words camera obscura have the same meaning; camera later became the familiar name of the photographic instrument. The modern photographs help us see the effect, while his writing gives us a particular set of questions and tests.

Follow light from the top of the object. It continues through the opening and reaches the lower part of the screen. Light from the bottom reaches the upper part. The opening is where these paths cross, so the positions reverse. Moving the screen farther back spreads the same paths farther apart, making the image larger in this model. Each outdoor point sends light in many directions, but the small opening admits only a narrow selection. On the receiving wall, the points therefore keep a spatial relationship that we can recognise. The room gives us a way to make that relationship visible, before we try to explain the much less accessible structures inside an eye. An apparently surprising picture has become something we can explain point by point.

The crossing creates another problem: when light from several sources meets in one opening, does each source keep a separate path? Ibn al-Haytham gives us a test for that.


Three lamps, one opening

The drawing looks down on the arrangement. Several lamps stand outside a dark chamber in a row parallel to its door. A small opening admits their light, and a wall opposite receives separate bright spots. Ibn al-Haytham describes this arrangement in the first book of his Optics. We show three lamps so that each path is easy to follow. The colours and letters belong to our teaching diagram.

Lamp A is on one side of the opening; its spot appears on the other side of the receiving wall. Before I screen that lamp, decide which spot should disappear. The opening itself will stay clear.

Only spot three disappears. The other two remain. When I remove the screen, the missing spot returns. We can try a different lamp and check the same relationship again.

That result does more than tell us that a dark room needs light. It connects a particular place on the wall with a particular source outside. The paths meet at the opening, yet the light continues to distinct destinations. Screening one source leaves the others available for comparison.

This is an example of Ibn al-Haytham joining an arranged observation to a geometrical explanation. The apparatus lets another person check the claim rather than simply accept the author’s authority.

Once light can be followed from an object to a receiving surface, the next difficult question lies inside us: what receives it in the eye?


A new answer inside the eye

These three pages bring the human work back into view. The Arabic manuscript on the left contains a diagram of two eyes and the nerves leading away from them. The Latin manuscript in the middle belongs to Roger Bacon’s Perspectiva. At the right is the title page of a book Kepler published in one thousand six hundred and four. Between them lies a continuing problem: how can light arriving from many points outside give us an ordered view of the world?

Ibn al-Haytham argued that light comes from objects into the eye. That changed what an explanation of sight had to do. It had to connect the geometry of incoming light with the body’s receiving structures. His account still gave the crystalline humour, in the front part of the eye, a central role in sensation. The modern diagram below the books shows Kepler’s later answer, so we should keep the two accounts distinct.

The Arabic Book of Optics became available in Latin as De aspectibus. Bacon studied it in the thirteenth century, working with its geometrical treatment of sight. His manuscript is useful evidence of what serious reception involved: text and diagrams had to work together. A reader needed to understand what each line represented, and how the diagram supported the explanation. Copying the words was only part of that task.

Kepler entered an optical tradition that also included Witelo. His title explicitly names that earlier author. In one thousand six hundred and four he explained how the eye’s refracting structures form a picture on the retina, the light-sensitive surface at the back. Light from the top of an object reaches the bottom of that picture, much as it did on our room’s wall. Explaining this image still leaves the further question of how we perceive what we see.

Across these pages, receiving a powerful explanation also meant finding places where it could be developed or changed. The history matters because the questions remained open to further work. We can recognise a contribution precisely by asking what problem its author inherited, which answer he accepted, and where his own explanation took a different course.

An experiment asks whether an explanation survives a test; an engineer also needs a mechanism to work again and again. We now return to one thousand two hundred and six and a different workshop problem: al-Jazari’s clock must complete one action and prepare its next cycle.


Design a cycle that begins again

A clock that announces one interval has only begun its work. To announce the next, it must return its moving parts to a usable starting position. That is the problem hidden inside this painted elephant. Al-Jazari described the clock in his book of ingenious devices, completed in one thousand two hundred and six while he served the Artuqid court. The page we can enlarge is a real manuscript copy made in one thousand three hundred and fifteen.

Inside the elephant’s body, a bowl floats in water. A small hole admits water, and the filling bowl gradually sinks. Its movement draws a line that helps release a ball above. The ball’s weight turns a dragon-shaped mechanism, setting other actions in motion. In the full design, linked figures and sounds announce the interval, while the bowl is lifted, emptied and prepared to float again.

Our cutaway condenses that sequence so that the timing device and the return movement are easy to see. The intended interval is half an hour. The alternative times shown for larger and smaller openings are teaching comparisons: they illustrate why admitting water faster changes the cycle. They are not measurements recovered from this manuscript.

Imagine having to make the machine work for a whole afternoon. A bowl that sinks too soon advances every announcement. A line that catches may stop the next ball from falling. A bowl that rises without emptying cannot begin the same interval again. These are practical consequences of the design, and each gives a maker something specific to check.

What the book passes on is therefore more than an attractive silhouette. It makes a sequence of dependencies available to another builder: this motion must happen before that one, and the end must prepare the beginning. A successful description helps the next maker diagnose a failure as well as reproduce a working sequence.

Such work needs sustained support for skilled people. The next question concerns the arrangements that paid teachers and gave learners the time and place to continue their work.


Who pays? Who teaches?

Al-Jazari could explain his clock because he had time, materials and a patron who wanted the work written down. A learner who came later needed support too: a book within reach, somewhere to study, and a teacher able to recognize an error. Those needs connected scholarship with the economic and social life of a city.

Here is a Qur’an stand made in one thousand three hundred and sixty for endowment to a madrasa. Its inscription records a gift intended to serve learning. The Arabic word waqf means an endowment. A donor could assign the income of a shop or an estate to pay a teacher, maintain a building or provide books. The gift’s continuing income mattered because studying took time, including time when the learner was earning little.

This Ottoman diploma belongs to calligraphy. In one thousand seven hundred and ninety-one, Ali Ra'if Efendi wrote an authorization through which an approved learner could sign their own work. Ijazah means authorization; what it authorized depended on the subject and the relationship between teacher and learner. A calligraphy diploma and permission to transmit a particular book recognized different accomplishments.

Karima al-Marwaziyya taught al-Bukhari’s hadith collection in eleventh-century Mecca. One account describes a student copying seven pages from her text. When he was about to compare his notes with the original, Karima insisted on participating in the comparison herself. Her authority included responsibility for the words a learner would carry away.

In Damascus, Zaynab bint al-Kamal became a sought-after transmitter of hadith. Records of reading assemblies name her as the presiding authority, and younger scholars came to receive texts through her. These records connect a named woman, a particular work and the people present for its reading.

Their work belonged to hadith scholarship, with its own methods of authorization. Alongside the endowment and the calligraphy diploma, it shows how much depended on living relationships: somebody could provide the means to study, while somebody knowledgeable listened, checked and took responsibility for what was passed on.

That support could sustain more than one learner. In Cairo, Oxford and Cambridge, dedicated income helped make teaching continue beyond the lifetime of a donor.


Who funds a place to learn?

At Sultan Hasan’s complex in fourteenth-century Cairo, a waqf supported teachers and students in the four Sunni schools of law. Dedicated revenue sustained a place where teaching, study and residence could continue together.

Merton’s foundation statutes of one thousand two hundred and sixty-four provided for a community of scholars supported by estates. College comes through French from Latin collegium, an association. At Cambridge, Lady Margaret Beaufort founded a professorship of divinity in one thousand five hundred and two.

Its stipend sustained a teaching post. Chair comes through French and Latin from Greek kathedra, a seat. Cairo’s complex, Oxford’s community and Cambridge’s post had different rules, while each turned property income into time for learning. Comparing them helps us see a practical problem institutions had to solve.

A funded teacher also needed something worth teaching. Ibn Sina’s Canon lets us follow a book into the classroom.


How the Canon became a textbook

Ibn Sina wrote the Canon in Arabic in the eleventh century, drawing medical learning into a carefully ordered work. In twelfth-century Toledo, Gerard of Cremona translated it into Latin, giving readers who knew the author as Avicenna access to that organization. The Arabic and Latin printings here came centuries later, when the work already had a long life in teaching.

Its opening calls medicine ilm and the Latin renders that as medicina est scientia. Medicine concerns the human body in health and when health has been lost, so that health can be preserved or restored. That purpose gives the student a reason to study the body before choosing a remedy.

The Canon has five books. Book One sets out general principles. Book Two treats simple medicines; Book Three, diseases of particular organs; Book Four, conditions affecting the whole body; and Book Five, compound remedies. A student could place a particular illness inside this larger arrangement, then connect it with the appropriate discussion of treatment. Bologna’s statutes assigned selected parts of Book One in the first year, specifying chapters to include or omit.

Jacques Despars, a physician and university teacher in fifteenth-century Paris, spent over twenty years writing a commentary on parts of the Canon. Even its first definition required thought. If medicine includes practical work, why call it knowledge?

The distinction is between knowing how treatment should be carried out and actually carrying it out. Both theoretical understanding and knowledge of practical procedures can be taught; treating a patient then requires their application. The opening definition could therefore organize a classroom argument, rather than merely supply a sentence to memorize.

A medical reader had to connect a definition with practice. An astronomer reading a translated table had a related problem: did its numbers agree with the procedure the book explained?


When a translator checks the numbers

Ptolemy’s Almagest reached Arabic readers as al-Majisti and Latin readers through Gerard of Cremona’s translation. Its tables let an astronomer look up a result instead of repeating a long calculation. That convenience made accuracy especially important: one copied number might enter many later calculations.

An arc follows a circle’s edge; a chord joins the same two points in a straight line. A chord table connects the angle at the centre with the length of that straight line.

A reader needed the table and the explanation to work together. Suppose a worked example tells you how a value should be obtained, but the table gives a slightly different result. Copying the page faithfully would preserve the disagreement. Checking the calculation could expose it.

Historian Stefan Zieme compared Greek, Arabic and Latin tables and repeated the historical calculations. Some tables in Gerard’s Latin tradition appear to have been recalculated to agree more closely with Ptolemy’s written examples. We cannot identify the calculator with confidence: Gerard himself or a collaborator may have done the work. The translation opened a book to new readers and became an occasion to examine the mathematical content.

The tables put the sky into numbers on a page. An astrolabe made mathematical relationships movable, so a learner could use them while looking at the sky.


The sky in your hand

The astronomical tables we have just considered required a reader who could turn numbers into a useful result. An astrolabe gives that reader another way to work: relationships in the sky become lines on a movable piece of brass. This one was made in Toledo by Muhammad ibn al-Saffar in one thousand twenty-nine or one thousand thirty. Its little projecting tips represent selected stars, and the whole openwork map can turn.

Beneath it is a solid plate engraved for an observer’s latitude. Travel north and the sky changes its position relative to your horizon: the celestial pole stands higher, and familiar stars follow different paths above you. The star map can remain the same while the local plate is exchanged. Our simplified model makes that change visible. The engraved object supplies the historical evidence; the model helps explain what the curves do.

For a time measurement, the observer first needs something measured outdoors, such as a star’s height above the horizon. The sighting arm helps obtain that angle. On the front, the star pointer is moved to the appropriate altitude curve, relating that observation to the instrument’s other scales. The brass does useful work because the observer knows how to connect a measurement with its representation.

This is also why an instrument can become difficult when it reaches a new place or an unfamiliar reader. Both the local setting and the instructions matter. A precisely engraved scale becomes useful only when a person knows which observation to bring to it.

The adjustment becomes easier to understand when the layers are separated: one part carries the stars, another carries the local horizon, and a pin lets them move together.


Take an astrolabe apart

In this Dresden museum photograph, another astrolabe has been displayed with its parts separated. The case at the back holds the stack. The solid plates carry the local horizon and altitude curves; the openwork rete carries the stars. Its Latin name means net, while one Arabic name, ankabut, means spider. The central pin holds these pieces together while allowing the star map to rotate.

The sighting arm, usually on the back, is the alidade, from Arabic al-idada. A maker has arranged these parts so that a user can measure an angle and compare it with a model of the sky. Their arrangement makes an astronomical calculation physically manageable.

Another surviving instrument shows what a new owner changed when the brass still worked, but its writing needed explanation.


One instrument, new readers

The plate belongs to an eleventh-century astrolabe made in al-Andalus and now preserved in Verona. Its original engraving is Arabic, but later users left other writing on the brass. The larger photograph is a published close view of the Cordoba plate. Its original inscription gives the city’s latitude as thirty-eight degrees and thirty minutes.

Imagine receiving this instrument when you know what a latitude plate does, but cannot easily read its labels. Replacing the whole object would be a large undertaking. Adding a city name you recognise could solve a much smaller, immediate problem.

Above the original inscription are faint scratches. Historian Federica Gigante says the Hebrew word appears to read Cordoba. We print her reading beside the photograph because the individual letters are difficult to distinguish at this scale. The clear type is a transcription, not a claim that every stroke is plainly visible on the brass. It lets us understand the proposed reading while keeping the actual evidence in view.

The next additions raise a different problem. Someone scratched thirty-six near the label; thirty-six and a half also appears. These are Western numerals, interpreted as attempts to amend the latitude. They do not make the instrument more accurate merely by being later. Gigante identifies conflicting, erroneous amendments on this and other plates. The new reader could make the city easier to recognise and still get its position wrong.

That is a valuable detail in a history of knowledge. Adaptation requires judgment, and judgment can fail. The object preserves evidence of use, translation and disagreement even though we cannot name every person who held it. To carry it forward responsibly, a user needed both readable labels and reliable values. Preserving an addition lets us study that decision; accepting its number would require another check against the sky or a trustworthy table.

In England, Chaucer made a related teaching decision: he would keep the instrument’s demanding operations, but explain them in the language his young learner could read.


Chaucer teaches the astrolabe in English

Chaucer addresses a young learner named Lowys, who has asked to understand the astrolabe. The prologue says his instrument is made for Oxford’s latitude, and that he knows little Latin. Chaucer’s solution is to explain its operations in English.

He explicitly names Greek, Arabic, Hebrew and Latin as languages in which this knowledge has been taught. English, he argues, can carry the same conclusions. He also makes a teacher’s choice about difficulty: begin with the parts, explain usable operations, and repeat a helpful sentence when the learner needs it.

The later drawing lets us find Aldebaran among the star pointers. Its name carries Arabic al-Dabaran, the follower. A translated lesson can retain words from earlier languages while making the instrument usable in a new one. The learner acquires the unfamiliar terms through an explanation of something he can actually do.

Chaucer’s reader needs a book as well as the brass. That lesson brings our translated texts and travelling objects together.

The pages carrying these lessons also moved through trade. Paper made in Europe could travel east and become the material of a Qur’an manuscript.


Paper travels in both directions

At the beginning of our journey, paper helped explain Baghdad’s busy world of books. Here the material returns from another direction. This Qur’an in the Khalili Collection was copied around one thousand three hundred and forty in Iraq or Iran on Italian paper. Its support had crossed the Mediterranean trade networks before the scribe began writing.

The study of the manuscript identifies a watermark bearing a cross. We cannot see that mark in this opening photograph: it is evidence recorded through examination of the paper. A watermark forms when wire on a papermaking mould leaves an area with fewer fibres. Against light, the thinner part reveals the design.

The sheet brings two histories together. Papermaking had travelled from China through Central Asia and Islamic lands into Europe. Italian workshops then developed a substantial export trade, selling paper to readers and scribes around the Mediterranean, including Muslims copying the Qur’an. A useful material could move through communities whose religious commitments differed. Its destination depended on merchants, available supplies and the choices of the people who bought it.

We have followed books, words and instruments into Latin and English. This manuscript makes the return journey tangible. Islamic societies were also places where imported materials acquired new uses.

Those journeys extended far beyond the Mediterranean. In West Africa, scholars and families built their own libraries around the needs of teaching, worship and local life.


Timbuktu’s living archive

Timbuktu grew where routes across the Sahara met the world of the Niger. Trade brought people and books into contact, and learned families copied, taught and composed works of their own. Their libraries held law, devotional writing and historical chronicles alongside arithmetic and astronomy. These subjects belonged to the life of an African Muslim society with its own teachers, patrons and questions.

Look at the circular diagram in this astronomy manuscript, copied in one thousand seven hundred and thirty-three. Its red and dark lines help a reader follow the explanation of a moving heaven. The book belonged to the Mamma Haidara Library, one of the family collections through which scholarly work remained available across generations.

This arithmetic commentary places a chart beside its verbal explanation. A learner can follow an operation in stages and compare a result with the example. Hisab means calculation or reckoning. Such a book could support the sustained work of learning a method, with a teacher explaining where a student had lost the thread. We have already seen a diagram make algebra understandable and a table help an astronomer calculate. Here those teaching possibilities are part of West African intellectual life.

Keeping a collection is a responsibility that can suddenly become dangerous. During the crisis of two thousand twelve to thirteen, as armed groups occupied Timbuktu and threatened its heritage, Malian owners and librarians decided to move manuscripts away. Abdel Kader Haidara and SAVAMA-DCI helped organize the evacuation. Families had to entrust their collections to others, accepting the risk of moving fragile pages because leaving them in place also carried a risk.

The manuscripts travelled in metal chests and in stages to reduce attention. Some journeys used donkey carts or canoes before vehicles could carry the chests onward. There was no single dramatic departure: the operation continued over months. The people doing this work needed local knowledge, coordination and the confidence of families whose history was in those boxes.

The images we have been studying come from a Library of Congress collaboration that began in December two thousand four. They therefore predate the evacuation. A separate HMML project digitized the Mamma Haidara collection between two thousand fifteen and two thousand twenty-three. Cataloguing and conservation connected these later efforts with the collections and their custodians.

A digital page lets us study from a distance, while the original still needs suitable storage and care. Here amanah an entrusted responsibility, has a human weight: families and librarians acted so that another generation could still encounter the work.

In nearby Djenne, another kind of knowledge survives through maintenance and teaching. The mosque takes us from caring for pages to learning with earth, timber and other people.


One prayer, two buildings

Djenne in Mali and Demak in Java offer two ways to make a place for Muslim prayer. Their communities share the orientation towards Mecca, while the buildings draw on different materials and inherited skills. Look first at what a builder would have to understand in order to keep each structure standing.

At Djenne, sun-dried earth bricks are covered with mud plaster. The projecting timber gives access for maintenance. The present Great Mosque was rebuilt in one thousand nine hundred and seven, within a much older tradition of earthen construction. Its survival requires renewed work as weather wears the surface. A wall made from earth asks its builders to know the material: how to prepare the mixture, apply it and repair it when rain has damaged the protective layer.

That knowledge can pass from an experienced mason to a younger worker through doing the job together. A written description can name the ingredients, while practice teaches how the plaster should feel and how it behaves on the wall. The mosque’s appearance therefore carries a history of repeated labour as well as an architectural design.

At Demak, the eye goes upward to the three roof levels. The mosque’s own architectural account describes thin wooden shingles and a roof carried by columns, including four principal teak supports. A carpenter has to understand how those supports carry a tiered structure and how its pieces meet. The form belongs to Javanese building traditions through which Muslim communities made a locally recognizable place of worship.

The common direction also looks different from these two places. On a modern globe, the initial bearing towards Mecca is about seventy-three degrees from Djenne and two hundred and ninety-four from Demak, measured clockwise from true north. These calculations help us understand the geographical problem; the historical alignment of either building needs its own local evidence.

A shared religious practice gave builders a common purpose, and each community answered with skills suited to its setting. The result made Islamic life part of the material and social world of the place.

The same relationship between a shared inheritance and local knowledge appears in writing. On Java and in the Ottoman world, writers adapted Arabic letters to languages of their own.


Jawi and Ottoman Turkish

These two pages use writing systems based on Arabic, but their languages are Malay and Ottoman Turkish. That distinction matters whenever familiar letter shapes make a page look immediately readable. A script supplies signs for writing; a language supplies words and the relationships between them. Writers could inherit the signs and adapt them for sounds that Arabic did not have.

The large letters make one small decision visible. Malay Pasai begins with a P sound. In standard Jawi, that sound is written with a letter shaped like Arabic fa', carrying three dots above. Ottoman Turkish uses a different sign for P: the shape of ba' with three dots below, as in Piri. Readers of these languages learned which sound each sign represented.

The Malay manuscript was copied in Semarang on Java in one thousand seven hundred and ninety-seven. It carries the story of Pasai, a kingdom in northern Sumatra. The work’s movement from Sumatra’s history to a copy made on Java reminds us that Malay writing connected places across the archipelago. The people copying and reading a book could live far from the events it described.

Its title includes hikayat, a Malay word related to Arabic hikaya, a story or narrative. The title preserves a linguistic connection, while the language of the work lets Malay readers encounter a history in Malay. Adapting a script widened the kinds of knowledge that could be written for those readers without requiring every subject to be expressed in Arabic.

Now the island of Paros in a later copy of Piri Reis’s Book of Navigation. The coastline and compass roses belong to a book written for maritime knowledge. A reader had to connect named places and written descriptions with the shore and sea a sailor might encounter. A chart made part of that information visible, while the accompanying text could explain details that a line on the page could not convey by itself.

Malay narrative and Ottoman navigation put inherited letters to different tasks. Their readers were connected with a wider written world through recognizable scripts, borrowed words and circulating books, and they made that inheritance useful within the languages and activities of their communities.

We can now bring these different journeys together. What made it possible for knowledge to remain useful as it entered another language, another place or another generation?


What made these journeys possible?

These journeys belonged to a connected civilization of cities, courts, workshops, schools and households. Arabic gave many scholars a shared written language, while translation opened work to other readers. Patrons and markets helped pay for books; teachers explained them; craftspeople made the instruments and sheets; families kept collections available.

Islamic societies developed knowledge through that combination of people and practices. We saw Muslims, Christians and Jews participate in different settings, with opportunities and constraints that changed across time. Worship gave some work a practical purpose, including questions about time and direction. Medicine, trade, government and curiosity created other demands.

For a moment, think about which experience in these stories felt familiar: reading signs that you once found strange, hearing an explanation that finally made sense, or trusting someone to help you check a difficult step. Some of you may have embraced Islam recently; others have been learning and practising for years. Our shared faith does not give us all the same language or history.

A learner in Mecca, a scribe on Java and a family in Timbuktu belonged to different worlds within a shared religious life. As Muslims, we share this inheritance across those differences. There is room here to recognize something of your own experience without expecting every life to follow the same path.

The pages and buildings we have seen give that history substance: an explanation someone could follow, a form a community could adapt, and a trust another generation could receive.

With those people and journeys in mind, I would like to close with the prayer for knowledge with which we began.


Rabbi zidni ilma

Rabbi zidni ilma.. My Lord, increase me in knowledge.

The prayer gives us a fitting place to end because every journey we have followed depended on someone who still had something to learn. Hunayn returned to a translation; a student checked a text with Karima; a carpenter learned how to carry a roof; a librarian accepted responsibility for a collection. Knowledge grew through work shared with other people, and through care for those who would come afterwards.
