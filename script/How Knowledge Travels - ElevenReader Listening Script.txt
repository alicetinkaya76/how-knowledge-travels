How Knowledge Travels: Islam in Different Cultures

Dr. Ali Çetinkaya


How Knowledge Travels: Islam in Different Cultures

As-salāmu ʿalaykum wa raḥmatullāh. I’m Ali Çetinkaya. Welcome. We meet as Muslims with different languages, cultures and journeys into the faith. We share something important, while bringing different experiences to it.

Tonight, I want to follow a simple question. How does knowledge reach someone who could not use it before? A book may arrive in a town, but its words are in another language. A useful instrument may reach a new owner, but nobody has explained how to use it. Moving the object is only part of the journey.

Think of medicine. When someone is ill, a book on a shelf cannot help by itself. Someone must understand its words, judge its advice and know how to apply it. Teaching and careful checking belong in that story, alongside the person who first wrote the book.

We will begin in Baghdad in the 9th century, when scholars were searching for older books. We will follow some of the work into Arabic, then meet readers using Arabic works in Latin and other languages. Our journey will also take us to Muslim communities in Africa and Southeast Asia, where learning served their own needs.

We will look at real pages and objects. We will try a calculation and follow light through a dark room. We will meet teachers, makers and families who kept learning available. You do not need to know the names or read these languages. I will introduce what we need as we go.

By Islamic civilization, I mean societies shaped by Islam. Muslims and people of other faiths lived and worked in these societies, bringing different kinds of knowledge.

Our first object is a map. Find a coastline you recognize. Which way round were you expecting the world to be?

South is at the top. Turning the map puts north at the top, which is more familiar to many of us. The places have stayed the same. What changed was how easily we could recognize them.

Al-Idrīsī, a Muslim geographer, completed this geography in 1154 for Roger II, the Christian king of Sicily. We are looking at a later copy. Their work gives us an early glimpse of learning across religious and language differences. A different-looking page can hold something another person will find useful.

Some of those journeys are already part of the English we speak.


You already speak this history

Zero, algebra and algorithm are ordinary English words. They also carry clues about people learning from other languages. In my project Riḥlat al-Kalimāt, the journey of words, I follow clues like these.

One of these words carries a person’s name. Which one would you choose?

Algorithm comes from the name al-Khwārizmī, a scholar who wrote about calculation in 9th-century Baghdad. Algebra comes from al-jabr, part of a book title. Zero has a longer journey connected with Arabic ṣifr, صِفْر.

The word is a clue, rather than the whole explanation. Knowing where algebra’s name came from does not yet show us what a learner could do with it. His algebra and his work on calculation were different books; the words travelled by different routes. We will return to the actual calculation later.

For now, these familiar words give us a reason to look behind the names. How did someone make an unfamiliar idea clear enough for another person to learn?

Looking carefully at earlier lives also has a place in our shared faith.


Travel, and look

Fa-sīrū fī l-arḍi fa-nẓurū. So travel through the land and look.

These words come from Qur’an 3:137. The verse asks people to reflect on earlier communities and what became of them. The passage concerns peoples who denied God’s message and the consequences they faced. It is an invitation to look and consider the meaning of what we find.

A beautiful page can catch our attention. Looking more closely may reveal a problem: a reader cannot understand its language, or part of the text is missing. Then the page becomes more than something to admire. We begin to ask what people had to do to learn from it.

Caring for that learning brings us to amānah: something placed in our care.


Knowledge is a trust

Amānah means a trust: something we receive with a responsibility to care for it. Knowledge can be such a trust. We need to understand it well enough to use it carefully and explain it honestly.

Four actions will help us follow that responsibility: receive, test, add and pass on.

Receiving brings earlier work within reach. Testing means asking whether the words and reasons deserve our trust. Adding may mean correcting a mistake or finding a better explanation. Passing on means helping another person learn.

These actions were often connected. A translator who found a better copy could correct an earlier version. A teacher could make a difficult idea clear. Neither task was finished simply because a book had arrived.

Rabbi zidnī ʿilmā. My Lord, increase me in knowledge.

Let us begin with receiving: why were readers in Baghdad looking for old books, and why were many of them in Greek?


Why look for Greek books?

Baghdad became the capital of the Abbasids, a Muslim ruling family. By the 9th century, people there were seeking books from several older traditions of learning. They had practical reasons to care. Doctors wanted to understand illness. People needed calculation for trade, land and inheritance. Others wanted to study the sky and the reasons things happen.

Greek books offered a large store of earlier work. For centuries, writers around the eastern Mediterranean had used Greek to explain medicine, mathematics and astronomy. Later teachers studied those explanations and discussed them with their pupils.

Galen gives us one example. He was a Greek-writing doctor in the Roman world in the 2nd century. His books described the body, illness and treatment. They also explained why he accepted one account rather than another. A later doctor could learn from those arguments and ask whether they were right.

The language was an obstacle for readers who did not know Greek. Some Christian scholars already worked between Greek and Syriac. Syriac is a form of Aramaic used by many Christian communities for study and worship. Their knowledge helped books reach readers in Arabic.

Greek was an important source, but it was not the only one. Learning from India and Iran also reached Arabic readers. Nor did writing in Arabic mean simply repeating older authors. Scholars asked their own questions and wrote new works.

The point of finding these books was to gain something people could learn from and use. A title alone could not do that. The contents had to become understandable.

This medical page lets us see what a Greek work looked like after it became available to Arabic readers.


A medical book finds Arabic readers

The writing is Arabic, but the work began with a Greek medical author. Dioscorides, a doctor in the 1st century, wrote about materials used as medicines. Later readers translated and studied his book because knowing a name was not enough. They wanted to know what a material was and how it was used.

Here a physician works with vessels beneath the text. This illustrated page was made in 1224, in Iraq or northern Jazira, possibly Baghdad. It comes centuries after the first Arabic translation work. It shows a later reader meeting that medical tradition in an Arabic book.

The other image belongs to a Greek copy made around 512. Its illustration shows a different scene. These are not pictures of the same passage. We are comparing two ways an old medical work was presented to readers.

A translator had to do more than replace one set of letters with another. Plant names could be difficult. A name familiar in one place might mean little to a reader elsewhere. A clear sentence also needed someone who understood the subject. A reader needed to connect the words with a material they could actually recognize.

Several people helped prepare and improve Arabic versions of such books. One was Hunayn ibn Ishaq, a Christian physician and translator working in 9th-century Baghdad. His medical knowledge and his languages belonged to the same task: helping a reader understand what an earlier doctor meant.

Why have we chosen this page? It makes the result visible. A reader who could study Arabic could now enter a medical discussion written long before, in another language. That opened the work to teaching and further questions.

Yet the illustration cannot show us all the effort needed before that reader could open the book. A translator first needed pages complete enough to understand.

Hunayn left an account of that difficulty: he knew the title of a book he wanted, but finding its pages was another matter.


Ḥunayn searches for a missing book

The book was Galen’s On Demonstration. Demonstration here means proof: showing why a conclusion follows from its reasons. Hunayn wanted the complete work, rather than a few pages with a famous name attached.

The eye diagram introduces Hunayn as a doctor and writer. It comes from a later copy of his own work on the eye. His search account concerns a different book, the one about proof.

Another scholar had already looked hard for a complete Greek copy. Hunayn searched too. In his letter, he names northern Mesopotamia, Syria, Palestine and Egypt, as far as Alexandria. In Damascus he found about half the book. Parts were missing, and the surviving sections did not run in order.

Imagine reading a reasoned explanation with several pages missing. The last sentence may still give you an answer. But somewhere earlier, the author explained why you should accept it. Without those pages, you cannot follow the whole argument. A phrase such as “as we showed earlier” sends you back to an explanation you no longer have.

Even a perfect knowledge of Greek cannot replace words that are no longer there. Hunayn’s first problem was therefore access to the text, before the problem of translating it.

The books he sought were copied by hand. Each copy was a separate object, kept somewhere by someone. A searcher needed to learn where it was, travel there or arrange access, and examine what had survived. Reaching another city did not guarantee finding a complete book.

Hunayn also says that he lost the books he had collected through his life and travels. A collection took years to build, one book at a time. Replacing it meant more than buying paper. It meant finding the texts again. Travel, skilled work and materials all cost something before the next reader could even begin to study.

Hunayn records making portions available in Syriac while identifying parts and gaps. Later readers could use what had been recovered and know that something remained to be found.

That is the human difficulty behind this map. The pages a scholar could recover helped decide what the next generation could study. Recording a gap could also keep a search going.

And when better copies did appear, they could change a translation that readers were already using.


Ḥunayn returns to his own translation

Hunayn tells us what that meant in another work by Galen, On Sects. This book explains different schools of medical thought: the different ways doctors tried to understand medicine.

He had translated it into Syriac when he was about 20. A physician had asked for it, and Hunayn had one Greek copy available. He later described that copy as very faulty. A new language could make its words easier to read without removing the faults already in the source.

At about 40, his pupil Hubaysh asked him to correct the Syriac. Hunayn now had several Greek copies and more experience. Where should he begin: improve the Syriac wording, or check the Greek copies first?

He began with the Greek. He compared the copies and prepared a better text. Then he checked his Syriac against it and corrected the translation.

There were two questions. What had Galen written? And had Hunayn expressed it well in Syriac? The second question depended on the first.

Several copies could disagree. Hunayn still had to judge which wording made sense of the passage. Counting copies alone would not settle that question: two might share the same error. Experience with the subject helped him make that judgment.

Suppose a sentence in one copy has lost a few words. It may still be possible to turn it into smooth Syriac. The result could sound clear while carrying the wrong connection between ideas. Another Greek copy might preserve the missing words. Comparing it gives the translator a reason to change the later version.

Checking the words did not prove that every medical idea in the book was right. It gave readers a better account of the ideas they needed to examine. They could question Galen’s reasoning with a clearer view of what he had said.

The photograph shows another Galen work, On Simple Drugs, in Syriac beneath later writing. It makes that language visible. It is not the particular On Sects copy described in Hunayn’s letter.

A few years after the correction, Hunayn translated On Sects from Syriac into Arabic for Muhammad ibn Musa, a patron who supported translation in Baghdad. Arabic readers could begin from work that had already been compared and repaired.

The story gives trust a practical meaning. Hunayn returned to his own earlier translation when he had better evidence. A pupil’s request, more copies and further study all helped make the book more useful. Later learners gained time: they could start studying the argument instead of repeating the whole search.

So far, we have heard Hunayn describe his work. A short medical saying will now let us see a thought moving between languages.


One thought, two languages

The saying opens the Aphorisms, a collection of short medical statements linked with Hippocrates, an ancient Greek medical writer. Its first thought is simple: life is short, while learning the medical craft takes time.

The Greek begins, Ho bios brachys, hē de technē makrē. The Arabic says, al-ʿumr qaṣīr wa-l-ṣināʿah ṭawīlah.

First, find the words for a short life. Then follow the words for the craft.

Greek technē and Arabic ṣināʿah mean a skilled practice here. An English translation might use the word art. But hearing art today may make us think of painting. This is a medical book. The skill it is talking about is learning and practising medicine. A medical student needs knowledge and practice. Recognizing the names of illnesses does not yet mean knowing how to treat someone. The saying puts that long learning task in view.

That small choice matters before the reader reaches the next line. If we mistake the kind of skill, we misunderstand the whole saying. A useful translation helps the reader begin with the right idea.

These real pages were made long after Hunayn’s lifetime. The Arabic manuscript’s record attributes its translation to him. We are comparing two versions of the same saying, rather than treating these two physical pages as his own copies.

The visible gain is clear: a person reading Arabic can understand a thought written earlier in Greek. The letters have changed; the medical lesson can still be followed.

Yet a good translation on one person’s desk would not automatically put a book into another learner’s hands.


A translation needs a book

Someone had to make that extra book. Each handwritten copy needed a writing surface, ink, a pen and a person able to use them. Knowing a text existed was different from being able to obtain it.

A learner might borrow a copy and return it after reading. They might buy one already made, or pay a scribe to make another. A scribe was a person who wrote books by hand. The last choice needed materials and hours of skilled work, as well as a source to copy from.

Look at these two Qur’an pages. One is parchment, prepared animal skin. The other is paper, made from fibres. They come from different centuries. Both had to provide a surface on which the writing could remain clear.

A pen had to be cut well. The page had to take the ink properly. The scribe also needed to keep every line in order. Missing one line could damage an explanation just as surely as losing an older leaf.

Folded sheets form small groups. Thread joins the groups, and a cover protects them. Keeping the leaves together helps keep the thought in order. Bound books already existed before Islam; Islamic makers developed their own bindings and ways of preparing pages.

These tools show why making knowledge available cost more than the price of an idea. Someone had to turn the words into a readable object that could be checked and used.

Before ordering that work, a reader faced another question: which books existed, and which one should they ask for?


The bookseller’s list

Ibn al-Nadim, a Baghdad bookseller, helped readers answer that question. Around 987 or 988, he compiled the Fihrist, a large survey of books and their authors. It brought together work in many subjects and from different communities.

A reader cannot request a book whose existence they do not know about. A list can make that first connection. It can tell you that someone has written about a problem you want to study, even if the book is not on the nearest shelf.

The list was not a promise that every title was complete or available for sale. It was a way of finding out what to search for. Knowing what to request could save a learner a long, fruitless search. Hunayn’s account has already shown us how much work could remain after that first step.

This printed page comes from an edition made in 1871. It preserves Ibn al-Nadim’s discussion of paper. The names include Jaʿfarī, Ṭalḥī and Ṣīnī. Ṣīnī means Chinese. His account links paper production in Khurasan, a region east of Baghdad, with China.

A book professional needed to know about the words and about the sheets carrying them. The trade brought readers, copyists and writing materials into contact. Warrāq, وَرَّاق, names work in this world of paper, copying and books.

That is why a page about paper belongs inside our story of learning. Finding the right text was only useful if people could also supply a copy.

The paper names lead beyond Baghdad, to the workshops and routes that kept writers supplied.


Paper travelled through a network

Papermaking began in China. By around 800, paper was being made in Baghdad. Its journey involved both sheets that people could buy and skills that people could learn.

A shipment could bring a town a supply for a time. A workshop needed fibres, water, equipment and trained workers. If people could keep making paper nearby, writers would not depend only on the next arriving shipment.

That practical difference matters to a reader waiting for a book. A copyist needs another sheet, and then another. More books mean more demand for a writing surface.

The dates on this map mark different kinds of evidence. A surviving letter tells us paper was being used. A report about a workshop tells us something about manufacture. The lines join places; they do not show one uninterrupted journey or a single date when everyone adopted the craft.

The network also branches. The Kashmir photograph records craft continuing farther east. Later we will meet European paper used for a Qur’an in Iraq or Iran. Sheets moved in more than one direction.

A supply of paper helped people copy and teach books. But what made a particular explanation worth returning to?

A book by al-Khwārizmī will let us follow the difference between being given an answer and understanding why it works.


Algebra: from a rule to a reason

Al-Khwārizmī worked in Baghdad in the 9th century, around the same time as Hunayn. His algebra book did more than give answers. It helped a learner see why a method worked. He named inheritance, trade and measuring land among its uses. Different everyday questions could share the same mathematical shape. Understanding one method could therefore help a learner solve another problem.

Here is his example. A square and ten roots make thirty-nine. Root means the unknown side of the square. In our modern symbols, x squared plus ten x equals thirty-nine. He wrote the problem in words. The moving pieces on our screen are a teaching model of his reasoning.

The central square has an unknown side. The extra area is ten copies of that side. Together, all the pieces have area thirty-nine. We need an arrangement that makes the unknown side easier to find.

Split the extra area into two strips. Each strip is five wide and x long. Put one beside the square and one below it. We have only moved the pieces, so their total area remains thirty-nine. The new shape is almost a larger square. One corner is missing.

That corner is five across and five down. What is its area? And if we add it to our picture, what must we do to the thirty-nine on the other side? Take a moment to decide.

Five times five gives twenty-five. Add twenty-five to the picture and also to thirty-nine. Both sides must stay equal. Thirty-nine plus twenty-five is sixty-four. Our completed square has area sixty-four, so each side is eight long. But that side includes the strip five wide. Take five away from eight. The original side is three.

We can check it in the original problem. Three squared gives nine. Ten threes give thirty. Nine plus thirty is thirty-nine. The answer fits.

Now the written rule has a reason. Halve ten to get the strips. Square that half to fill the corner. Add the corner to thirty-nine. Find the side of the completed square, then take away the strip. Each instruction belongs to a change that we have seen.

The square also tells us which answer this picture is seeking: a positive length. We should not ask the drawing to stand for every kind of equation in modern algebra. Its strength here is that an area gives a visible reason for the steps in this particular problem.

Someone learning this method can now do more than remember an answer. They can explain the steps, find a mistake and try the method with new numbers. Adding the corner without changing thirty-nine would break the equality. The picture helps us see the error. A teacher can also change the numbers and ask the learner to explain the new steps.

The Arabic manuscript keeps the diagrams with the explanation. The word algebra comes from al-jabr in its title. Algorithm follows another route, through al-Khwārizmī's name and his work on calculation. A book could carry both a useful method and a way for another reader to check it.

The drawing has helped us check a calculation. But suppose the question is about something we see: why does light turn a picture upside down? We need to look at what light actually does.


How can light turn a scene upside down?

This room gives us a visible problem to explain. The bed is upright, but the outdoor building appears upside down on the wall. Its roof is below its windows. Most daylight has been blocked. A small opening admits enough light to form the image.

Each point outside sends light in many directions. The small opening lets only a narrow group of paths enter the room. That keeps light from different outdoor points separate enough to make a scene. With the whole window open, those paths would spread across one another on the wall, and we would lose the clear picture.

The second photograph shows a larger projection made at Brown University in 2023. A lens helped brighten it. Look for blue sky below the building and inverted trees. These are modern demonstrations. They show an effect we can inspect; they are not photographs of a medieval experiment.

Ibn al-Haytham was a scholar born in Basra, in today's Iraq, who worked in Cairo in the 11th century. He used dark spaces and small openings to study light. Al-bayt al-muzlim means dark room. The Latin name camera obscura also means dark room. Our word camera comes from that Latin word for a room. We are using the modern images to make a problem in his work easier to follow.

Follow light from the top of the outdoor object. Its straight path passes through the opening and reaches the bottom of the screen. Light from the bottom reaches the top. The paths cross at the opening, so the picture is reversed. Move the screen farther away in our model: the paths spread farther apart, and the picture gets larger.

Keep the outdoor scene and its image separate in your mind. The building itself has not turned over. The change comes from which outdoor point sends light to which part of the wall. The opening selects the paths that make that relationship visible.

This example shows a particular gain from investigation: we can explain the reversal and predict a change in the image. Islamic scientific work included making and testing explanations, as well as learning from older books. We can also work backwards. Light low on the wall came from a point high outside.

Following light through a room is easier than explaining everything about human sight. It gives us one manageable part of the larger problem. But several paths enter through the same opening. We still need to ask whether they remain separate after they cross.


Three lamps, one opening

Ibn al-Haytham describes several lamps outside a dark chamber. Their light passes through one opening and makes separate spots on the wall. Our drawing uses three lamps and three colours to help us follow their paths.

Each lamp makes a spot on the opposite side. Before I cover lamp A, predict which spot will disappear. I will leave the opening clear and the other lamps shining.

Spot three disappears. The other spots remain. When A is uncovered, its spot returns. Each light still reaches its own place after crossing the others.

Why cover a lamp rather than the opening? Covering the opening would remove every spot. We would learn that light needs the hole, but little about the crossing. Removing just one source lets us keep the other paths in view.

Here is an experimental method we can follow. Ask a question, predict a result, change one thing and compare what happens. Restore the lamp and check the connection again.

This is more useful than simply hearing that a famous scholar studied light. We can follow his reason for accepting a particular answer: the separate spots can still be tested one by one. It is one clear investigation within a much longer history of experimental work.

The paths remain separate in the room. Now follow one into an eye. Where does its light arrive, and what part of the eye lets us see?


A new answer inside the eye

Light can enter an eye, but that fact alone does not explain how we see. These pages show people working on that next problem. They kept useful questions from earlier writers while changing parts of the answer.

The Arabic page comes from Ibn al-Haytham's Book of Optics, Kitāb al-Manāẓir. The diagram shows eyes and the nerves leading away from them. He explained sight through light arriving from objects. He gave an important role to the clear structure near the front of the eye that we call the lens. That is different from the explanation we learn today.

His book reached Latin readers as De aspectibus, a book about sight. They also knew him as Alhazen. Roger Bacon, an English scholar in the 13th century, studied this optical work. He is a different Roger from the king of Sicily we met at the start.

Look at the lines beside Bacon's writing. A diagram helped readers follow a claim about where light went. They could point to a line, ask what it represented, and check whether it fitted the explanation. Translation made that earlier discussion available; reading it carefully created more questions.

Johannes Kepler was an astronomer who also studied light. His book here was printed in 1604. Its title refers to Witelo, another writer in the Latin optical tradition. Kepler explained how light bends inside the eye and forms an upside-down picture on the retina, the light-sensitive surface at the back.

Our small modern model shows that answer. Light from the top of an object reaches the bottom of the retinal picture. The picture's position is one problem; how a person understands what they see is another.

This is why the books belong together. An Arabic explanation gave later readers arguments and drawings they could examine. Bacon and later optical writers worked with that inheritance. Kepler's answer changed an important part of it. Learning from a book could lead someone to go beyond it.

A written explanation also has to work for someone who was not there to watch. Could a reader rebuild a device from its instructions? For that question, we step back from 1604 to 1206 and an engineer's clock.


A clock that starts again

A clock has to do its job more than once. Announcing an interval is only half the problem. It must also get ready to start again. Al-Jazarī was an engineer for the Artuqid rulers in Diyarbakır, in today's Turkey. Their court supported the work of skilled makers. He completed his book of ingenious devices in 1206. The illustrated copy here was made later, in 1315.

The elephant attracts our attention. Inside it, a bowl with a small hole floats in water. Water slowly enters the bowl until it sinks. That movement pulls a line and helps release a ball above. The ball’s weight turns a dragon-shaped part and sets other movements in motion. Figures and sounds announce the interval.

The mechanism also lifts and empties the bowl. It can then float and fill again. Our cutaway follows fill, release and reset. Half an hour is the intended interval. The alternative times in the model illustrate the effect of changing the opening; they are not historical measurements of this manuscript’s clock.

If the bowl comes up without emptying, the first announcement may work beautifully. The next cycle will fail. Following the sequence helps a maker find the fault.

The reset is easy to miss because the public result is the announcement. A useful instruction has to reveal that less visible work as well. Otherwise another maker might copy the appearance without understanding how the machine becomes ready to perform again.

The book lets another maker learn the hidden part of the performance. A spectator sees a ball fall; a maker needs to know how the bowl becomes ready again. Without that explanation, a beautiful machine could stop when the first person who understood it was no longer there.

Al-Jazarī had a court behind his work. Books and lessons elsewhere also needed someone to provide materials and time. Let us move from the ruler's workshop to the people who supported students and teachers.


Who pays? Who teaches?

A student needed more than a wish to learn. Someone had to provide a place, a book and a teacher's time. The stand, diploma and teaching records show different people taking care of those needs.

The Qur’an stand was made in 1360 as a gift for a madrasa, an institution where students studied with teachers. Its inscription records the gift. The stand was one gift to help people study. A donor could also set aside income from a shop or estate to pay for teachers, rooms or books year after year. This continuing support is called a waqf, or endowment, and could last after the donor's lifetime.

That is the stand’s place in our story. Access meant more than having a book somewhere in the city. Continuing support could help learners reach books and teachers over time. Particular institutions offered different subjects and conditions; one example cannot tell us how every madrasa worked.

The Ottoman calligraphy diploma was written by Ali Ra’if Efendi, a calligrapher, in 1791. It allowed an approved learner to sign their work. Ijazah means authorization. Its scope depended on the skill or text involved. Permission to sign calligraphy was different from permission to pass on a particular book.

We also see a teacher checking the words herself. Karīma al-Marwaziyya taught hadith in Mecca in the 11th century. Hadith are reports of the Prophet’s words and actions. She taught the collection made by al-Bukhari, a 9th-century scholar who gathered hadith. When a student copied seven pages from her text, she insisted on taking part in comparing them with the original.

Later records from Damascus name Zaynab bint al-Kamal, another respected hadith teacher, as the authority in reading sessions. Scholars came to receive reports through her. These women’s expertise mattered to what students could responsibly pass on. Checking the words and recording the teacher’s name connected a later lesson with work someone had examined.

A gift could support more than one lesson. What happened when people set aside income to keep a whole place of study running?


Who funds a place to learn?

In Cairo, Sultan Hasan's 14th-century complex used an endowment to support teachers and students. Income from property helped keep lessons and accommodation available.

Merton College in Oxford also had rules for supporting a community of scholars. Its foundation dates to 1264. At Cambridge in 1502, Lady Margaret Beaufort, a royal patron, founded a paid teaching post. We call such a post a chair.

There is a small word journey here too. College comes through Latin collegium, a group of people. Chair comes through words for a seat in Greek and Latin. These word histories are different from a proven history of one institution copying another.

What the examples share is a simple need. A teacher needs time to teach, and students need the means to stay and learn.

Supporting a teacher is one part of the job. What should the teacher teach first? A well-arranged book could help answer that question.


How the Canon became a textbook

Ibn Sīnā was a Muslim doctor and philosopher from Central Asia. In the early 11th century, he arranged a large body of medical knowledge into a book that teachers could use step by step.

Its Arabic title is al-Qānūn fī al-ṭibb, the Canon of Medicine. Qānūn means a rule or body of principles. The title describes his plan for the subject. The book's later authority grew as people studied, taught and discussed it.

Here is the separate Arabic-to-Latin example we promised. Al-ṭibb ʿilm: medicine is knowledge. The Latin opening says medicina est scientia. The next words explain the purpose: to keep health when we have it and restore it when it is lost.

Gerard of Cremona was a translator from Italy working in Toledo in the 12th century. He translated the Canon from Arabic into Latin. The printed pages here are later Arabic and Latin copies, dated 1593 and 1507. They let us compare the opening; neither is Gerard's own working manuscript.

The five books connect general principles, simple drugs, diseases of particular organs, conditions such as fevers, and mixed medicines. A student could connect a particular illness with a wider explanation of the body, then find the relevant discussion of treatment.

Why did this make a difference in Latin-reading Europe? Teachers already had works by Galen and Hippocrates. The Canon gave them a large, connected medical system in one work. They could build a course around it, return to earlier principles and discuss why a treatment followed from a diagnosis.

Bologna's medical rules assigned parts of Book I in the first year. In Paris, the doctor Jacques Despars wrote explanations of the Canon between 1432 and 1453. Such a book explaining another book is called a commentary. These are records of what teachers and students actually did with the text.

An Arabic work by a Muslim scholar had become part of how other communities trained their doctors.

A course plan is still a large picture. What could a student learn on a particular page? Let us take two problems that need very little introduction.


What could a student learn from the Canon?

A broken bone is the first problem. This page, from a later English translation, describes bringing the separated parts together, supporting them with bandages and keeping the injured part still.

The steps have a reason. The bone has lost its connection, so the treatment aims to hold the parts together while they heal. A student can connect the action with the problem. The paragraph also gives a dietary theory that differs from modern medicine; we are reading an account from its own time.

The second problem is knowing what a visible sign means. Ibn Sīnā connects sandy material in cloudy urine with a stone. Elsewhere he warns that food, an unwashed container or a sample left standing can change what the doctor sees.

That is more than learning a symptom by heart. Before trusting the sign, the student must ask what else could have produced it. The book brings observation, explanation and a decision together.

Gerard translated this medical book, but his interests reached beyond medicine. In Toledo he also worked on an Arabic version of a famous astronomy book. There, checking the meaning of words was only part of the task.


When a translator checks the numbers

That book was the Almagest. Ptolemy, an astronomer working in Alexandria around 150, wrote it in Greek. Arabic scholars translated and studied it. Gerard then made it available in Latin.

Astronomers needed repeated calculations. A table saved time by keeping results ready to look up. Think of using a reliable price list: you do not calculate each price from the beginning every time.

Here the table connects an angle with a length. An arc follows the curved edge of a circle. A chord is the straight line joining the same two points. Those relationships helped astronomers work with geometrical models of the sky.

A table also lets one error travel a long way. A copied number may look neat and still be wrong. Anyone who uses it carries the mistake into another calculation.

The explanation beside the table gives a way to check. Repeat its steps, then compare the answer with the printed entry.

When historian Stefan Zieme repeated the old calculations, some numbers in the Latin copies agreed with Ptolemy's written examples better than the Greek and Arabic numbers he compared. Gerard or someone helping him may have checked and corrected them. We cannot tell who did that work.

The translator's work could therefore include checking the mathematics. A useful book had to give readers dependable numbers as well as understandable words.

Tables put long calculations on a page. Astronomers also put mathematical relationships into an object they could carry outside and turn in their hands.


The sky in your hand

This object is an astrolabe: a model of the sky that can be held in the hand. The maker, Muḥammad ibn al-Ṣaffār, worked in Toledo in 1029 or 1030. The thin tips of the open metalwork point to named stars.

The star map turns over a solid plate. Why have two layers? The stars are not seen against the same horizon in every place. Travel north and the point around which they seem to turn stands higher above you. A plate made for one latitude cannot describe every other place equally well.

Latitude means how far north or south a place is. Our model lets us change from Baghdad to Istanbul. Keep an eye on the horizon line while I change the place.

The star map can stay the same, while the local plate changes. On the real instrument, a user could put a different plate underneath the turning map.

An observer could also measure a star's height with the sighting arm on the back. The instrument linked that observation to its model. With further instruction, the user could work out information such as the time.

This is the useful idea to keep: the mathematics travelled in the object, but using it somewhere new still required the right local setting. Carrying the same brass instrument to another city did not automatically make it ready.

The photograph on the next page separates those layers. It will make the job of each part easier to see.


Take an astrolabe apart

Here is another astrolabe, displayed in Dresden with its parts apart. The case holds everything. A solid plate gives the local horizon. The open star map turns above it.

The Latin name for that turning map is rete, a net. Its Arabic name is ʿankabūt, a spider. The central pin holds the layers while letting them turn.

The sighting arm measures an angle. Its English name, alidade, comes from Arabic al-ʿiḍāda.

The clever part is that the jobs are separate. The user can change a local plate without rebuilding the star map.

A new owner may have the right plate and still face another problem: the writing on the instrument is in a language they cannot read.


One instrument, new readers

On this astrolabe, later readers added writing they could use. Made in 11th-century al-Andalus, in the Iberian Peninsula, it is now in Verona in Italy. Arabic engraving appears beside later Hebrew writing and Western numerals.

The Arabic inscription names Córdoba and gives its latitude as 38°30′, or 38.5 degrees. We have just seen why a latitude belongs on a plate. It identifies the local setting represented by the engraved lines.

Above the Arabic, historian Federica Gigante reads a faint Hebrew addition as apparently saying Cordoba. The clearer letters beside the photograph show her proposed reading of those faint marks.

For a reader more comfortable with Hebrew, a familiar city name could help identify the right plate. Reusing an existing instrument would then become easier. The object could serve another community through a small addition to its surface.

The numbers tell a different story. Someone added 36, and 36 and a half also appears. Gigante interprets these as attempts to correct the latitude. These changes made the latitude less accurate. An addition could therefore make a label easier to understand while another addition made the information less accurate.

This is why the additions matter. One helps another reader find a city; another gives less accurate information. Making something easier to read and making it more reliable are not always the same thing.

An instrument made in an Islamic society remained useful to people reading other scripts. Its later history includes their work and their mistakes. We can recognize that shared engagement without assuming every change was an improvement.

An added label helps someone recognize a part. A learner who needs to operate the whole instrument also needs an explanation.


Chaucer teaches the astrolabe in English

Geoffrey Chaucer, the English poet, wrote a different kind of help in 1391. His Treatise on the Astrolabe addresses a boy called Lowys. The boy has an instrument for Oxford, wants to learn, and knows little Latin.

Chaucer writes in English. He begins with the parts, then gives manageable lessons. He is willing to repeat an explanation if that helps the boy remember.

This is more than changing the language. He has to choose what to explain first and how much the learner can follow at once. The lesson succeeds when Lowys can use the instrument himself.

Find Aldebaran on this later drawing. The star's name comes from Arabic al-Dabarān, the follower. A learner can keep that older name while learning the operation in English. A method does not have to lose every trace of its earlier life to reach someone new.

Chaucer began with the needs of a particular learner. Almost 200 years earlier, another boy's education had connected an Italian family with the knowledge of a North African port.


From Béjaïa to a merchant’s classroom

That boy was Leonardo of Pisa, better known as Fibonacci. His father worked with Pisan merchants in Béjaïa, in today's Algeria, and brought him there to learn calculation.

In that Arabic-speaking port, Fibonacci learned Indian numerals. He later described the experience in his Latin book, Liber abbaci, a book about calculation. He first wrote it in 1202 and revised it in 1228.

We have met translations made from one book to another. Here, a person learned by moving between communities. What he took home included a method he could explain to other people.

Look at 25 and 205. The same 2 means two tens in the first number and two hundreds in the second. Zero keeps the empty tens place in 205. The position of a sign changes its value.

Fibonacci calls zero zephirum, a name connected with Arabic ṣifr. The word travelled, but the important thing to learn was how the arrangement worked. Units, tens and hundreds could stay in their proper places through a written calculation.

Later Italian abbaco schools taught practical calculation, often in the local language. Abbaco here means this kind of arithmetic teaching, not simply a lesson with a counting frame. Merchants' children needed to work with prices, money, measures and shared profits.

Try the cloth problem. Three lengths cost 12 florins. How much should five cost?

Divide 12 by 3: each length costs 4 florins. Multiply 4 by 5: the price is 20. The quantity changes but the rate stays the same. Buyer and seller can both check how the answer was reached.

The Treviso Arithmetic of 1478 shows this kind of teaching in print. Our cloth problem is a modern example of the method. A port, a Latin book, local classrooms and print all helped calculation reach new learners.

Those Italian trading towns also supplied a material we have already met: paper. Its journey did not stop when it reached Europe.


Paper travels in both directions

This Qur'an was copied around 1340 in Iraq or Iran on Italian paper. Earlier, we followed papermaking westwards through Islamic lands. Here, sheets travelled eastwards and became the surface for Qur'anic writing.

A study of the manuscript identifies a cross watermark. It is not visible in this photograph. That finding comes from examining the paper. A watermark forms when wire on a papermaking mould leaves a thinner area in the sheet. Light passing through the paper makes the design easier to see.

The mark concerns the manufacture of the sheet. The scribe used that sheet to copy the Qur'an. Those are two different parts of the object's history, brought together by someone obtaining a suitable writing material.

The page makes an important part of the story visible. Islamic communities were still choosing materials and using them for their own purposes. This was continuing exchange, with goods and ideas moving in several directions.

For us, the words of the Qur'an are familiar. The Italian sheet beneath them may be less expected. A scribe brought those histories together by choosing a useful writing surface.

We should also follow books beyond the Mediterranean ports. In West Africa, Muslim families built libraries around the subjects their own communities wanted to learn.


Timbuktu’s living archive

Books also had long lives in Muslim communities far from the Mediterranean ports. Here we are in Timbuktu, in present-day Mali, south of the Sahara. Trade routes crossed there, and families built libraries for their own learning, worship and work.

They copied and wrote books about law, history and faith. They also studied arithmetic and astronomy. We have chosen these pages to see what people learned inside the libraries. Their value goes beyond being beautiful old objects.

The astronomy manuscript was copied in 1733. Its circle represents the turning of the heavens. The book explains, among other subjects, how movements of stars can help identify the beginning of seasons. A pattern that returns in the sky gives people a way to organize time.

The next work is by al-Rasmuki, an 18th-century scholar who explained arithmetic. His charts and worked problems teach addition, subtraction, multiplication and division. The Library of Congress identifies it as a student text used in Timbuktu and North Africa. A learner could practise a method and check the steps. A chart gives a teacher and learner something they can follow together. They can stop at a difficult step and check how the answer was reached. That is the same kind of teaching need we met in the merchant's classroom.

These are examples of African Muslims doing intellectual work: learning, explaining and writing for other readers. The surviving pages give us evidence of that work. Losing the books would also mean losing much of the evidence.

During the crisis of 2012–2013, armed groups occupied Timbuktu and threatened its heritage. Families and librarians moved collections away. Abdel Kader Haidara, a Malian librarian, helped organize the work with SAVAMA, a local group of manuscript owners and librarians.

The books travelled in metal chests. Some journeys used donkey carts or canoes, then vehicles took collections onwards. This needed people who knew the routes and could earn a family's trust. Handing over a family library was a serious decision.

Keep the dates separate. The Library of Congress project behind these pictures began in 2004, before the crisis. The rescue came in 2012–2013. Later, a US manuscript library, known as HMML, digitized the Mamma Haidara collection between 2015 and 2023.

A digital copy lets someone far away read a page. The original still needs care. Here, the trust we called amanah means protecting something that another person may need to learn from.

Nearby, in Djenne, people protect another inheritance by working on it together. Their mosque needs skills that each generation must learn.


One prayer, two buildings

At Djenne, knowledge is also passed on through hands at work. Look at the mosque's earth walls, then compare the roof at Demak in Java, in present-day Indonesia. Both buildings serve a prayer familiar to us. Their shapes are different because their builders worked with different materials and local skills.

The present Great Mosque of Djenne was rebuilt in 1907. It belongs to a much older tradition of building with earth. Sun-dried bricks carry a layer of mud plaster. The pieces of timber projecting from the walls help people reach the surface for repairs.

Rain and weather wear that surface. Keeping the mosque standing means repairing it again and again. Someone must know how to prepare the mixture and put on the protective layer. A new worker can learn beside an experienced mason: how the plaster should feel, how it spreads, and when more work is needed.

At Demak, the roof rises in 3 levels. Wooden shingles and columns give the building a different form. A carpenter has to understand how the roof's weight is carried and how the pieces fit together. Knowing a method also means knowing which material it suits. Local knowledge makes a place for Muslim worship.

That is why we compare these mosques. The differences show people meeting the same religious need with the skills of their own communities. Teaching those skills helps the common purpose continue. A photograph of a building can also be evidence of work shared across generations.

The qibla gives us another way to understand the difference. Both communities turn towards Mecca, but Mecca lies in different directions from their homes. Our modern map gives about 73 degrees from Djenne and 294 degrees from Demak, measured clockwise from true north.

These are teaching calculations. They do not tell us exactly how either historic mosque was aligned. Their purpose here is simple: facing one sacred place does not mean pointing the same way on every local map.

The walls, roofs and directions can differ while the prayer remains shared.

Writers faced a similar question. How could something shared across the Muslim world also serve the language of a particular place?


Jawi and Ottoman Turkish

These pages show a similar kind of adaptation in writing. Both use Arabic-based letters, but one is in Malay and the other in Ottoman Turkish. Sharing letters does not make two languages the same. English and Turkish also share Latin letters, yet their words and grammar are different.

The Malay page tells the history of Pasai, a kingdom in northern Sumatra. This copy was made at Semarang, on Java, in 1797. A reader on another island could meet that history through Malay sentences.

The other page comes from the Book of Navigation. Its author, Piri Reis, was an Ottoman sailor and mapmaker in the 16th century. The later copy shown here describes Paros, an island in the Aegean Sea.

Now compare the two large letters. Pasai and Piri both begin with a P sound. Arabic has no letter for that sound, so writers needed another way to record it. Malay Jawi uses a familiar shape with 3 dots above. Ottoman Turkish uses a different shape with 3 dots below.

The marks are small, but the result is useful. A writer can record a place name as people say it, and a reader can connect that name with a familiar place. The Malay title includes hikayat, from Arabic hikaya, meaning a story. A borrowed word became part of a work whose language remained Malay.

The Ottoman page shows coastlines and compass roses. The words name places and explain things a drawing alone cannot tell a sailor. Adapted letters helped carry practical knowledge in a language those readers understood.

These pages bring the cultural question close to home. A connection with Arabic could grow alongside people's own languages. Shared faith could support several ways of writing, learning and remembering.

We can now put the journeys together. What had to happen for knowledge to become useful in another community?


What made these journeys possible?

At the start, we asked what happens when people need knowledge that someone else already has. The journeys have given us several answers. A useful idea does not reach a new reader through movement alone. People have to make it understandable, check it and help someone learn to use it.

Hunayn first needed a readable book. He searched for missing pages and returned to his translation when better copies arrived. That work changed what a later student could understand. A bookseller and a copyist helped put those words within another reader's reach.

Al-Khwarizmi then gave us a reason for a calculation. We could see why the answer worked, rather than only follow an instruction. Ibn al-Haytham asked a different question. His arrangement of lamps let someone test what happened to separate paths of light. We chose these examples because the steps are open for another person to examine.

A book also needs readers who can keep learning from it. The Canon gave medical teachers a shared plan. Chaucer chose English for a young learner. Fibonacci made methods learned in North Africa available to readers elsewhere. Families in Timbuktu protected books that held their own learning. Those choices helped new readers join the work of learning.

This also answers the second half of our title: Islam in different cultures. We saw Muslim communities building, reading and teaching in different ways. Christians, Jews and others also took part in the societies through which this knowledge travelled. Their work did not all have one purpose or one language.

For us as Muslims, a shared faith can sit alongside different languages and family histories. The roofs at Djenne and Demak make that visible. The Malay and Ottoman pages give it words. People made a common inheritance part of local life.

That leaves us with a prayer for the understanding we still hope to gain.


Rabbi zidnī ʿilmā

Rabbi zidni ilma. My Lord, increase me in knowledge.

This prayer gives our journey a fitting end. Hunayn could correct his work because he still had something to learn. Karima checked a student's pages because passing on knowledge carried a responsibility. A builder repaired a wall, and librarians protected books for people they might never meet.

Each story has helped us see that responsibility in a different form. The inheritance reached us through human effort: through someone learning a language, checking a line, explaining a method or caring for an object.

We have met that work across many places and centuries. As Muslims with different languages and lives, we can recognize a shared place within this history. There is room for gratitude, curiosity and further learning.

May what we learn deepen our understanding, and may we remember those whose work made that learning possible for us.
