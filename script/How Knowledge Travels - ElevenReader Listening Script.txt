How Knowledge Travels

As-salamu alaykum wa rahmatullah. I’m Ali Cetinkaya. It’s good to be with you.

Imagine opening a medieval map of the world. You expect north at the top. This map invites you to turn that expectation around.

South is at the top. Africa is above the Mediterranean and Europe is below it. The page comes from a later copy of a geography completed in one thousand one hundred and fifty-four by al-Idrisi, a Muslim scholar working for Roger the Second, the Christian king of Sicily. Rotate the map in your mind and picture north at the top. The coastlines haven't moved. What changes is the habit you bring to reading them.

Today we’ll ask what happens when a book, a method or a tool reaches people who bring different habits to it. What do they learn from it, and what can they do with it that its earlier readers couldn't?

A map carries habits for reading the world. Before we look at words, let’s follow a set of signs that changed the way Europeans wrote numbers.


From Bejaia to the abbaco schools

Begin on the North African coast. In the late twelfth century, a boy from Pisa learned arithmetic in Bejaia, in present-day Algeria. His father worked there with Pisan merchants. The boy, Fibonacci, tells us about his education at the beginning of the Liber abbaci. A later manuscript copy includes the famous rabbit problem. The same Indian place-value system, taught to him in the Arabic-speaking Mediterranean, let readers work out prices and divide profits. What does an empty tens place need? A zero. Turn twenty-five into two hundred and five and the two now counts hundreds instead of tens. In his Latin book the Arabic sifr becomes zephirum.

What was an abbaco school? A lay school where boys, often from merchant and artisan families, learned to calculate in their own language. Teachers gave them problems in prices, money, weights and partnership. Some teachers made a living from fees. Others elsewhere in Italy were paid by their town. The Florentine chronicler Giovanni Villani counted six schools in the late thirteen thirties, with roughly a thousand to twelve hundred boys. His count shows how established these schools already were. Lay education had a longer history.

Suppose three lengths of cloth cost twelve florins. Five lengths will cost twenty. Calculate the price of one length first. Then multiply by five. This is the kind of proportional problem an early printed handbook from Treviso put into Venetian. A lesson that travelled across the Mediterranean had become a book a local merchant could read.

The calculation travelled. So did the words people used to teach it.


You already speak this history

In Rihlat al-Kalimat, my project on the journey of words, we follow how names travel between languages. Consider zero, algebra, algorithm, zenith, nadir, azimuth, alkali and syrup. Some help us calculate. Others locate a point in the sky or name a substance.

All eight came to English through Arabic. Which of them, though, carries the name of a person? Make a choice before hearing the answer.

The word is algorithm. It comes from the Latinized name of al-Khwarizmi. Zero comes from sifr, and algebra comes from al-jabr. Each word carries a history. In a little while we’ll solve one of his problems ourselves.

Zenith and nadir describe opposite points, directly above and directly below. Azimuth tells us a direction around the horizon. Your telescope or navigation software may use the same vocabulary. Alkali comes from a word for ashes, and syrup comes from sharab, a drink. Those are clues to older uses of the terms.

Think of the route we have just seen. Much of Europe called the digits ‘Arabic numerals’. Medieval Arabic-speaking scholars wrote about ‘Indian reckoning’. Each name gives us a different point of view on how the notation travelled. We can do the same with a word. Follow its form and then ask which book, instrument or task made that form useful. We’ll do that as we move through the talk.

A word can carry a trace of what somebody learned from another person. As we follow that process, I’d like to keep two actions in view. A verse names them simply.


Travel, and look

Fa-siru fi l-ardi fa-nzuru. “So travel through the land and look.” In Surah Ali Imran, verse one hundred and thirty-seven, the words that follow ask us to observe what became of earlier people who denied the message. The verse connects movement with attention to what we find.

This evening we will travel through books and objects. We can look at a manuscript, compare a translation, or test an explanation of light. Each asks us to pay attention to the evidence in front of us.

Learning brings a responsibility for what we pass to somebody else. There’s an Arabic word that names that responsibility.


Knowledge is a trust

That word is amanah, a trust. A book or an idea can be entrusted to us. What do we do with it?

Receive. This copy makes a Greek physician’s work readable in Arabic.

Test. Special photography helps us check old writing beneath newer ink.

Add. The algebra diagram helps another reader see why a rule works.

Pass on. A donor makes a book available to learners. Each step needs somebody’s care.

Three places where knowledge changed

Here are three places to keep in mind. The first is Baghdad. There, translators compared copies of medical books and worked out how to explain them in Arabic.

In Cairo, Ibn al-Haytham used a dark room to find out what light does. You will see what he changed and what he could measure.

In Toledo, Arabic works found readers who studied in Latin. We'll see astronomy and medicine turn into material a teacher could explain in class.

A city that drew books and translators

Our first verb is receive. And our first stop is Baghdad.

An illustration shows people preparing a medicine from honey beneath two trees. Their gestures help a reader follow the work.

In the Greek copy made around five hundred and twelve, the woman holds the mandrake while a painter depicts it and Dioscorides writes. The Baghdad page from one thousand two hundred and twenty-four illustrates another passage by showing the work of preparing a medicine from honey. Both are later copies of a work first written in Greek in the first century.

The Arabic title, Kitab al-Hasha'ish, means Book of Herbs. The Latin title names the subject as materials of medicine. How did a Greek work acquire new readers and illustrations in Baghdad?

In the early Abbasid centuries, Baghdad became a major centre for translation into Arabic. Greek medicine and mathematics circulated alongside Persian astronomical traditions and Indian methods of calculation. Patrons included caliphs and other members of their courts. People who could read and write across languages came from different religious communities. Greek material could be translated through Syriac or compared with Syriac while being prepared for Arabic readers.

Imagine you are making this medicine from written instructions. You need to know which substance a word names and what the person making the medicine is meant to do with it. The translation has to give a reader enough to act. A picture can make one action easier to recognize, and the words can explain details that the picture leaves open.

The Muslim philosopher al-Kindi urged readers to welcome a true insight wherever it came from, even if the person who found it lived far away and spoke another language. Here we can see what that attitude asked of people in practice. Someone sought the work, someone supported the translation, and a copyist and illustrator made the result available to new readers.

When we look at this page, those hands matter as much as the journey on the map. A reader could study an account made available through the work of people from different times and places.

These folios show what later readers received. To see what it could take to find a text, let’s follow one physician’s search for a book by Galen.


The man who travelled for half a book

The physician was Hunayn ibn Ishaq. He was a Christian doctor and one of the major translators of the ninth century. A later copy of his Ten Treatises on the Eye includes an anatomical diagram with lines connecting parts of the eye to their names.

A teacher could use a diagram to give a student a place to look while explaining a part of the eye. The drawing and the explanation do related work. In this case we can also ask how the books that a physician learned from came into his hands.

Hunayn describes his search for Galen’s On Demonstration in a letter.

He tells us that he searched in northern Mesopotamia, Syria, Palestine and Egypt as far as Alexandria. Listen to that list as a search for several pieces of information. Where had a copy been seen? Who could let him examine it? Was the piece he found the part he needed?

Suppose you have completed that search. How much of the work would you expect to have in your hands?

In Damascus he found about half the book in fragments. He recorded that the sections were incomplete and out of sequence. That helps a later reader judge exactly how much he had recovered. Think of a reader asking a question that the missing half might have answered. A report that says where the material runs out gives the next reader a clearer place to resume the search.

We also learn something from who did the work. A Christian physician could work for Muslim patrons in Baghdad. A later reader could learn from his scholarship because people with different languages, backgrounds and resources made a place for that work. Naming the people who sustained it shows how a text reached the next reader.

Put al-Kindi’s ideal beside Hunayn’s account. The wish to learn from someone far away had to become a search for particular words on particular pages.

The map tells us where Hunayn looked. His notes tell us how much he found. For another book, he left a record of what he did when copies disagreed.


How Hunayn repaired a translation

Hunayn tells us how he revised his translation of a different work by Galen, a short introduction to schools of medical thought called On Sects. At about twenty, he translated it from Greek into Syriac for a physician. He had just one Greek manuscript and later described it as faulty. About twenty years later, his pupil Hubaysh asked him to look at the translation again. By then, Hunayn had several more Greek copies to work with.

The writing in this photograph includes the Syriac translation of another Galen work, On Simple Drugs. The colors come from processing images made under different kinds of light. They help us distinguish the older medical text from writing added when the leaves were reused. This surviving page shows how much work it can take to read a text that travelled in Syriac.

Back to On Sects. Suppose Hunayn finds a mistake in the version he made at twenty. Where should he look first? Would you open his Syriac translation or go back to the Greek copies? Consider whether you would begin with the Greek copies or the Syriac translation.

Hunayn went back to the Greek. He compared what the copies said and used them to establish a revised Greek text. Then he corrected his Syriac translation against that text. A difficult sentence might have come from his first Greek copy. He could also have misunderstood a good Greek sentence. Working in this order helped him tell those possibilities apart.

He also made an Arabic rendering for Muhammad ibn Musa. A decision he made about the Greek could reach a physician reading in Arabic years later. We can follow the decision because Hunayn wrote down how he worked. His letter gives us the name of the book, the state of his first copy and the steps he took with more evidence.

This is one way to test what has reached us. When a copy or translation seems difficult, check what the source actually says, and keep a record of what you used to decide.

Hunayn checked his translation against other copies. Ibn al-Haytham gives us a different way to check a claim. We can set up an experiment and see where the light goes.


Why does the world turn upside down?

We have checked the wording of a translated book. Now imagine checking a claim about the world itself. A photograph of a dark studio shows a building projected onto an interior wall, with blue sky beneath it. The outdoor view has arrived upside down.

This room was built at Brown University in two thousand twenty-three. The organizers covered a window and fitted a lens into one opening. The lens gathers the light to make the image brighter. The room itself becomes an optical instrument. Only a little daylight enters through the opening.

Ibn al-Haytham worked with dark chambers in eleventh-century Cairo. His Book of Optics explained vision in terms of light coming from objects into the eye. The dark chamber allowed him to investigate the paths that light follows. In one arrangement, several lamps cast separate patches of light. Covering a lamp removed its patch, making individual paths available for comparison. A small hole alone can produce an image. Let’s use that simpler arrangement to follow its geometry.

The upper point on the object is gold. The lower point is green. Imagine a ray of light from the upper, gold point passing through the opening. Will it reach the upper or lower part of the projection surface?

The ray from the gold point passes through the opening. It keeps travelling downward and reaches the lower part of the screen. The green line reaches the upper part. Now imagine moving the projection surface farther away while leaving the object and opening fixed.

The image grows because the screen meets the same rays farther apart. The Arabic phrase al-bayt al-muzlim means dark chamber. The later Latin name camera obscura also means dark room. Photography eventually shortened that name to camera.

A picture gives us something to observe. How do we turn that observation into a test someone else can check?


What makes the picture appear?

We have seen a picture appear inside a dark room. Now ask one question. Does that picture need the light coming through the opening? Keep the room and outside scene the same. Change only whether the opening is clear.

Imagine performing the test in the same room. Predict what happens when the opening is covered.

The projected picture disappears.

It returns when light can enter again.

Ibn al-Haytham described related physical tests with lamps. Covering one lamp removed its corresponding light spot. The other spots remained. Removing the cover brought its spot back. He could connect a particular source to a particular result.

That is the reasoning we are following. Ask a question, make one controlled change and use the observed result to explain what happens.

An artist can use that same geometry to decide where a point belongs on a flat drawing.


From a projected image to a constructed picture

Durer’s woodcut shows a string. It runs from a fixed point to the lute and passes through a frame. Where it crosses the frame gives a point in the drawing.

The frame acts as a drawing plane. Each point of the object has a line back to the same viewpoint. Marking those intersections one by one produces the outline of the object.

Latin perspectiva meant the study of vision. Renaissance artists developed procedures for constructing pictures, while optical books supplied geometrical accounts of sight.

In the dark chamber, light continues through an opening and forms an inverted image on a surface. In Durer’s construction, the artist marks intersections on a plane between viewpoint and object.

Leonardo’s study of how distant objects appear also has specific links to Ibn al-Haytham’s account of vision through air.

Let’s follow the readers who kept those optical arguments available, and the new questions they brought to them.


How does the eye make a picture?

Think of the dark room. Light enters at the front, and a surface receives a picture inside. An eye also admits light. Where does its picture form?

In eleventh-century Cairo, Ibn al-Haytham developed an account of sight based on light entering the eye from objects. His Arabic Book of Optics became available to Latin readers as De aspectibus.

Bacon read that Latin work and used geometry to investigate sight. Other readers, including Pecham and Witelo, reworked optical arguments for their own audiences.

Kepler later addressed Witelo’s optics. In one thousand six hundred and four he explained how the eye forms a real, upside-down picture on the retina. The retina is the light-sensitive layer lining the back of the eye. It receives the image.

The inherited question had received a new answer.

We have followed an explanation across languages. Now let’s follow a mathematical problem from Baghdad into new teaching.


Algebra: turn an instruction into a reason

We have watched people use light and geometry to explain vision. In Baghdad, al-Khwarizmi used geometry to explain a rule for finding an unknown number. His problem says, “A square and ten roots make thirty-nine.” Imagine a square garden whose side we do not yet know. The square’s area is the side multiplied by itself. Ten roots means ten copies of that side. We show his words in modern symbols, x squared plus ten x equals thirty-nine. His book explained the work in words. Notice that the problem begins with relationships between quantities. Once we recognize those relationships, the same procedure can be used with different numbers. Al-Khwarizmi’s preface mentions practical needs such as dividing an inheritance, trade, and measuring land. A reader could connect these procedures to decisions that affected property and everyday work. Explaining why the procedure worked made it easier for a teacher to pass it on.

Suppose the unknown side is x. We can split ten x into five x and another five x. Each strip has the same length as the side of the square and a width of five. We have rearranged the same total area. Nothing has been added yet.

Put one strip on the right and the other below the original square. Together the pieces nearly make a larger square. What is missing? Follow the lower edge of the right strip. The empty corner is five across and five down. Its area must be twenty-five.

Add that corner to the picture. To keep the equation true, add twenty-five to the thirty-nine on the other side. The completed square now has an area of sixty-four. What number multiplied by itself gives sixty-four?

Eight. The whole side is eight, including the added width of five. The original side is therefore three. We can check the answer against the original words. Three squared is nine. Ten times three is thirty. Together they make thirty-nine.

The Arabic manuscript preserves the explanation. The word al-jabr in the title travelled into Latin and later English as algebra. The Latin arithmetic manuscript carries the author’s name as Algorizmi. It eventually gave us algorithm. A title and a person’s name travelled with procedures readers could use.

We have used a drawing to make a calculation understandable. An astrolabe puts a different kind of mathematical drawing onto brass and makes it movable.


The sky in your hand

Suppose you need to tell the time outdoors and you can pick out a star. With an astrolabe and a clear sky, a trained observer could use the star's height to work out the hour. In some cities, such timekeeping also helped people determine prayer times. This instrument was made in Toledo in the Islamic year four hundred and twenty, which overlaps one thousand twenty-nine and one thousand thirty. The open brass layer carries little tips that point to stars. Turn that layer and the stars move together, as they appear to over the course of a night.

Under the star map is a plate made for an observer at a particular latitude. The pale curves on the model are a way of mapping positions in the local sky. Travel north from Baghdad to Istanbul and the North Star appears higher. The same brass star map can still be used. You change the engraved plate underneath. To use it, aim the sighting arm at the star and read its height from the rim. On the front, turn the star pointer until it crosses a curve at that height. The dial then helps you read the time.

Consider four star names: Deneb, Altair, Vega and Arcturus. Three passed through Arabic. Which one came from Greek?

Arcturus comes from Greek. Deneb carries the Arabic word for a tail. Altair preserves “the flying,” from a name for a flying eagle or vulture. Vega preserves “the falling,” from a name for a falling eagle or vulture. Reading the names brings us a little closer to how people found their way around the same sky.

Let’s see exactly how the brass pieces fit together so the stars can turn and the plate can be changed.


Take an astrolabe apart

A museum display in Dresden separates the pieces of an astrolabe. A central pin runs through those layers.

At the back is the case, or mater. The Latin word means mother, matching Arabic umm. It holds the layers and has a scale around the rim. The solid plates are called safa'ih. They carry engraved curves, like the plate of the Toledo instrument. These curves represent positions in the local sky. That is the part that can be exchanged when you move far enough north or south.

The open brass piece in front is the rete, the Latin word for net. One Arabic name, ankabut, means spider. The pin is called qutb. It holds the stack together while the net turns. That means the star map can move while the local horizon stays fixed, just as the real night sky appears to turn. On many astrolabes, the other side has an arm you can aim towards a star. The word alidade carries the Arabic name, al-idada.

A new observer could change a plate. Our next instrument shows something else a new observer could change: the writing on the brass.


One instrument, new readers

The same instrument could reach someone who read a different language. The Verona astrolabe gives us a concrete example. Imagine holding this brass instrument and looking closely at the writing on one of its circular plates.

Its maker worked in al-Andalus in the eleventh century. The original engraving is Arabic. Among its labels are the names of cities, so a user can find the plate suited to their location. The instrument also has lines connected with Muslim prayer times.

The name here is Cordoba. A reader familiar with Arabic could recognize the city and use the label to find the relevant setting. Another person added a second way to identify the city.

The scratches are much lighter than the original engraving. Historian Federica Gigante reads this word as Cordoba written in Hebrew. The plate still carries its Arabic label, and now it also has a label for someone who reads Hebrew. The user has made an existing instrument easier to read.

There are also lightly scratched Western numerals. Here we can make out thirty-six. Gigante thinks these additions were probably made for a reader of Latin or Italian. The exact dates of the later marks are unknown. The Arabic engraving came first, and subsequent readers added writing they could recognize.

We can follow the object through these additions even when the people themselves remain unnamed. The same piece of brass carried a useful way of representing the sky into new settings.

A new label could help a person read an instrument. In Oxford, Chaucer tackled the same problem by writing its instructions in the learner’s own language.


Chaucer teaches the astrolabe in English

You may know Geoffrey Chaucer from the Canterbury Tales. Around one thousand three hundred and ninety-one he also wrote instructions for a young reader named Lowys. The prologue says the boy has an astrolabe set up for the latitude of Oxford. That detail should sound familiar after the plate we looked at in Verona.

Chaucer wrote in Middle English. Some of the Latin material he used had travelled through Arabic. His prologue promises a lesson in plain words. Lowys can learn the language of the instrument by using it, as he might learn to find the letters on a compass. Chaucer begins by showing the reader what the pieces are called. Then he puts them to work. One lesson shows how to tell the time using the height of the Sun. It matters that the learner understands which piece turns and which scale to read.

An illustration in a later edition shows the name Aldebaran on the rotating star map. The name comes from Arabic al-Dabaran, “the follower.” The bright star appears to follow the Pleiades across the sky. What travels with an instrument might be a whole set of instructions, or one word that still lets us find the same star.

An astrolabe can help a person work out the time. What would it take for a machine to announce the time by itself?


How the elephant clock keeps time

Imagine hearing the time before you look up. A ball strikes a cymbal, a dragon turns and the figures on top of an elephant begin to move. That is what al-Jazari's elephant clock was designed to do. Al-Jazari described it in a book completed in one thousand two hundred and six at the Artuqid court. A manuscript copied in one thousand three hundred and fifteen depicts the clock’s exterior. Its written instructions explain the mechanism inside.

Imagine a bowl floating in water inside the elephant's body. A little water enters through a hole in its base. As the bowl gets heavier, it sits lower. The rate at which it fills gives the clock its interval. What happens if that opening is made bigger?

A wider opening lets the bowl fill faster, so it triggers the strike earlier. Al-Jazari aimed for a bowl that would fill in about half an hour. In a workshop that takes repeated testing. If it sinks too early every cycle, the error accumulates. By lunchtime you hear an announcement for a time you have yet to reach. A maker could compare one cycle against a reference, adjust the opening, and check it again.

As the full bowl sinks it tugs a line and releases a ball up above. The ball rolls towards the dragon, which pivots. As the dragon tips, the ball drops to strike a cymbal. The dragon's motion also helps lift and tip the bowl so the water inside spills out. The bowl floats again and the next half-hour begins.

Leonardo da Vinci also sketched water-clock components some three centuries later. The Royal Collection identifies water-clock components in those drawings, though the exact arrangement is uncertain. Lay the two images side by side in your mind. Each maker had to consider how the movement of water could give a reliable interval. Al-Jazari also shows a reader how a sequence of small motions can turn it into a public signal.

If you were asked to repair the clock, the picture would tell you where to look. The written sequence would tell you which motion you expected to see next.

We can recover the sequence because someone described the parts and someone kept copying the book. Let's look at the sheet that made that possible.


From sheet to book

These Qur’an pages were made centuries apart. One was written on parchment, prepared from animal skin. Another was written on paper, formed from fibres. Either surface can carry a sacred text. The scribe also chooses the size of the page, the spacing and the script.

Here are real Ottoman tools, made centuries after the medieval pen box. The knife renews a reed pen’s writing edge. The cutting slab steadies it, and the scissors trim paper. These objects help us picture the scribe’s work. The word qalam preserves Greek kalamos, meaning reed.

The bound format, or codex, had developed in antiquity. Muslim makers inherited a form already used in the Mediterranean world. Their workshops developed its use with paper, distinctive bindings, carefully designed pages and the trades needed to circulate books. Folded leaves form gatherings. Thread joins the gatherings. A cover protects them. Many Islamic bindings add a flap that closes over the exposed edge.

A reader can open that book at a chosen passage, compare two pages and add a note in a margin. Its physical arrangement supports the work of study. The same volume can pass through several generations of teachers and learners.

A ream comes ultimately from Arabic rizma, a bundle. Today we count sheets ready for a printer. A manuscript workshop also needed a dependable supply.

Paper travels in both directions

A craft can cross a border and later return as a product for sale. Fabriano’s makers used water-powered stampers and sizing, and marked their products. Such workshops supplied scribes before presses began ordering sheets in larger quantities.

How papermaking travelled west

The dates beside the places mark surviving evidence or recorded production. Each arrow links evidence from a different time. Paper began in China. A Sogdian merchant’s letter, written around three hundred and thirteen and found near Dunhuang, was addressed to Samarqand. By around eight hundred, workshops were making paper in Abbasid Baghdad.

Paper helped meet the needs of officials, scholars and book buyers. Those readers and their institutions also created demand. The famous story of captives teaching papermaking after Talas leaves the exact circumstances of transmission open.

Egypt was making paper by the end of the ninth century. In the twelfth century, al-Idrisi described paper made in Muslim-ruled Xativa and sold abroad. Finished sheets could travel with merchants. Local manufacture also required trained people, materials and power.

Italian mills supplied a growing trade. Administrative records, manuscript books and later printing all used paper. We have already seen a printed Bible emerge from that market.

In one thousand four hundred and ninety-five an English book printed by Wynkyn de Worde credited John Tate for its paper. Tate ran a mill near Hertford. England had imported paper before this local production. A mill’s date tells us when one supply existed, while the growth of reading involved schools, languages, income and institutions as well.

Tate needed clean water to power his machinery and suitable rags to feed it. The working mill depended on trade contacts as well as technical knowledge. Imported sheets continued to supply English printers alongside this brief local enterprise.

We can see the difference a teacher makes in science as well. Imagine a student working out a geometry problem from a diagram. She can copy every line and still benefit from a teacher who asks which measurement justifies the next step.

Think back to the elephant clock. The shop income in our example can help put a book within reach. A person who knows how a mechanism works can help a student decide what to measure. Access and explanation do different jobs, and a learner needs both.

The diploma names what a learner can do. The inscription records what a gift is meant to support. Let’s compare those gifts at the scale of institutions in Cairo, Oxford and Cambridge.


Who funds a place to learn?

A gift can support a learner in several ways. At Sultan Hasan’s complex in fourteenth-century Cairo, revenue set aside in a waqf paid teachers and supported students. Its deed describes teaching in the four Sunni schools of law. Some students also had places to live.

At Oxford, Merton’s one thousand two hundred and sixty-four statutes provided for a community of graduate fellows. Estates helped fund their study. The English word college came through French from Latin collegium, an association. A gift could keep supporting new learners after older members moved on.

At Cambridge, Lady Margaret Beaufort founded a professorship of divinity in one thousand five hundred and two. Its stipend sustained a teaching post. The word chair comes through French and Latin from Greek kathedra, a seat. The seat gave its name to the post.

Cairo’s gift sustained a complex, Oxford’s a scholarly community, Cambridge’s a post. These records let us compare how each place supported learning. Historians have debated possible connections between Islamic endowments and European colleges. Tracing such connections takes dated records of people and practices. The word histories shown here can be followed separately.

A teaching post could also be recognized in ceremony. What can a scholar’s gown tell us about these connections?


Graduation gowns and robes of honour

Robes can make a position visible. At Oxford, gowns and hoods marked academic rank. The university traces these forms to dress in medieval Europe. This print shows a doctor of divinity in one thousand eight hundred and fourteen.

In Islamic societies, a khila could convey honour. Here Robert Shirley wears Persian attire given by Shah Abbas. He represented the shah in Europe. We know the giver and the recipient. The ijaza we saw earlier records another kind of recognition, a learner’s authorization.

A clothing word travelled too. Arabic jubbah reached southern Italian as jupa, then French as jupe. Its meaning changed. French speakers now use jupe for a skirt.

The word route and the diplomatic gift are documented. A direct Islamic origin for European graduation gowns remains unproven. Similar clothing raises a question that we can investigate through records.

We have seen how people recognized a scholar’s position. What did those scholars mean by knowledge?


Knowledge means knowing a thing as it is

Compare these two definitions. Imam al-Juwayni, who lived in the eleventh century, defines ilm in al-Waraqat as knowing the thing known as it is. Two centuries later, a Latin sentence quoted by Jerome de Moravie says, Scientia est cognitio rei sicuti est. Knowledge is knowing a thing as it is.

The phrases follow a similar pattern in both languages. Knowledge has an object. Something is being known. The judgment should agree with how that thing actually is. If we think a paper’s watermark tells us who wrote on it, we need to check what the mark really records. It might identify the mould that made the sheet. That is a claim about paper production. Finding its writer takes other evidence.

This later copy contains al-Juwayni’s text and a commentary. Jerome names Johannes de Garlandia when quoting the Latin definition in a work about music. He goes on to classify kinds of knowledge. The two sentences show a similar standard in different teaching settings. A definition at the start of a book tells the learner what counts as an adequate answer. A convincing claim still has to fit its object, whether the lesson concerns law, music, or a measurement. To establish a translation between these particular texts, we would need a traceable source.

Consider what students learned. Al-Juwayni’s legal primer teaches interpretation and reasoning about evidence. Paris’s arts syllabus includes grammar, logic and works about nature. A medical book gives us a more direct connection.

Let’s open Ibn Sina’s Canon and see how readers encountered its definition of medicine in Arabic and Latin.


How the Canon became a textbook

Why did medical teachers put this book on the syllabus centuries after its author died? The Arabic edition was printed in Rome in one thousand five hundred and ninety-three. The Latin edition was printed in Venice in one thousand five hundred and seven. Latin readers already knew the work through centuries of copying and teaching.

The Arabic author is Ibn Sina. The Latin book calls him Avicenna. In the twelfth century, Gerard of Cremona translated his medical work from Arabic into Latin in Toledo. We can check what those readers encountered. The Arabic opening says al-tibbu ilm. The Latin opening has medicina est scientia. Both introduce medicine as knowledge of the conditions of the human body, in health and when health has been lost.

The order mattered. Book One begins with the body and the general principles of medicine. The other books go through simple drugs, illnesses of particular organs, conditions that affect the whole body, and compound remedies. Students had a framework that let them ask where a particular case fitted. Renaissance Italian teachers could lecture on that first book, and the French physician Jacques Despars wrote a Latin commentary that dwells on this very definition.

Despars describes Ibn Sina setting out a definition, raising a difficulty about it, and answering that difficulty. A classroom could start with the same question. If medicine includes practical work, what does it mean to call the whole subject a form of knowledge?

A medical teacher had to explain the words in a definition. What happens when a translator takes on a page of numbers that someone needs to calculate with?


How do you find a missing number?

A table can save you from doing the same calculation every time. Ptolemy used tables to help astronomers work with angles. This page comes from the book that reached Arabic readers as al-Majisti, and Latin readers as the Almagestum. We can read a little piece of it together.

Imagine two dots on the edge of a circle. The curved edge between them is an arc. A straight line joining the same dots is a chord. When an astronomer knew the angle at the centre of the circle, the table gave them the length of this straight line.

For half a degree, the table gives a chord length of zero point five two four. For one degree, it gives one point zero four seven. These values use the book’s scale, with a circle radius of sixty, and are rounded here into modern decimals. What if your angle is three quarters of a degree? It is halfway between the two angles, so we can try a length halfway between their two values.

That gives us about zero point seven nine. The book had a column to help its readers make just this kind of estimate.

When historians compared these Arabic and Latin copies, they found that some numbers had been worked out again in the Latin version. Its columns agreed with one another more closely, which helped readers spot slips made in copying.

For any table to help the next reader, someone has to keep the page safe and available. In Timbuktu we can see how families carried that responsibility over generations.


Timbuktu’s living archive

The astronomy table survives because readers copied and kept it. In Timbuktu, learned families did the same with whole libraries. The city stood in a network of West African trade and scholarship. Books on law and religion belonged in this world, and so did books about numbers and the sky.

The astronomy manuscript contains a circular diagram in red and dark lines. It represents the rotating heavens on a flat page. The copy is dated one thousand seven hundred and thirty-three. The Mamma Haidara Library kept this manuscript in a collection that includes both works used widely in Islamic scholarship and texts produced locally.

Now an arithmetic commentary, with a chart beside the words. Hisab means calculation or reckoning. A diagram in the first book helps a reader picture something moving. A worked example in this book gives a learner something to do in order, and a result to compare with their own. When these two pages are studied together, they make it easier to ask what was taught in West Africa and how a reader was meant to learn it.

The pages still need care. Ahead of Timbuktu’s occupation in two thousand twelve, many manuscripts were moved to Bamako for safety. Malian librarians and the association SAVAMA worked with library partners to digitize collections and describe individual items. The example is a Library of Congress facsimile from the Mamma Haidara collection. It can be studied at a distance. Its catalogue connects the page to the library, and the original still needs care. That is one way to understand amanah an entrusted responsibility. Each step requires somebody to be answerable for the page and the information that goes with it.

We have seen what West African families kept on paper. In nearby Djenne another kind of knowledge comes down through the people who build and maintain a mosque. We can set their building alongside one in Java.


One prayer, two buildings

Compare two mosques. Djenne, in Mali, has earth walls and projecting timber beams. Demak, on the island of Java, has a roof that rises through three tiers. Their materials and shapes reflect the building knowledge available to their communities.

What must a new builder learn? Think of one skill a builder would need.

In Djenne people gather to renew the mosque’s earthen plaster. Weather makes the job necessary, and working alongside others is a way to learn it. Someone can tell you the steps, and you still have to get the feel of the material in your hands. Demak’s three-tiered roof belongs to a Javanese architectural tradition. There, a builder needs a different set of skills, with different materials and forms.

Now picture the people inside at prayer. Both communities need the direction of Mecca, the qibla. Astronomers in Islamic societies developed ways to find it. Which direction would each community face?

From Djenne the initial bearing is about seventy-three degrees. From Demak it’s two hundred and ninety-four. Both are measured clockwise from true north along a great-circle route. These are modern calculations. The historical orientation of either mosque needs local evidence.

The shared question can travel, as the mathematics of direction travelled. The builder also needs knowledge of local materials, weather, and craft.

Let’s stay on Java’s north coast. The mosque uses forms familiar to Javanese builders. In Semarang a copyist makes a shared script work for a local language.


Jawi and Ottoman Turkish

The two mosques adapted a shared form. Let’s see something similar in the letters on these two pages. One page was copied in Semarang on Java. The other is an Ottoman chart of an island in the Aegean. Both writers use letters based on Arabic. They write in different languages.

Start with the P sound. In standard Jawi, Pasai begins with a letter shaped like Arabic fa' with three dots above. In Ottoman Turkish, the name Piri begins with a letter shaped like ba' with three dots below. You can hear the same P. The two communities marked it differently.

This is a story of the kingdom of Pasai in Sumatra, copied in Java for later readers. The book is written in Malay. Its title includes a word with an Arabic root. Hikaya means a story. In Malay, hikayat names a kind of traditional narrative.

Here is the chart of Paros from the Kitab-ı Bahriye. Piri Reis put names beside the features a sailor could recognize. See the islands and the lines that fan out from the compass roses. The book uses Ottoman Turkish to turn that picture into knowledge a sailor can consult. Malay readers could get a kingdom’s history, and Turkish readers could learn the shape of a coastline.

A script made these stories and charts legible to new readers. Let’s see what else a writer can make legible, from the name of a city to the placement of weights on a balance.


Three scholars and their methods

Different writers can give readers different ways to check what they learn. Suppose a traveller mentions a place you have never heard of. Take the entry for Ani in Yaqut’s geographical dictionary. The entry gives its pronunciation and locates the fortified city between Khlat and Ganja. Another traveller can compare those details with their own account.

Al-Khazini worked in Merv. In The Balance of Wisdom, this diagram shows a beam, its marks and the suspended pans. A final weight alone leaves out how the measurement was made. The diagram lets a student find where to hang the pans and what to hold fixed in a second trial. Its title includes mizan a balance.

Al-Samaw'al grew up in a Jewish family and later embraced Islam. He wrote algebra in Arabic. Think back to al-Khwarizmi’s square. Al-Samaw'al worked with expressions that have several powers of an unknown. The modern notation here shows one cube plus another cube making two cubes of the same unknown.

A dictionary can let a reader check a place. A diagram can let a learner reconstruct a measurement. A rule can be applied to another problem. Each writer decides what a learner needs to repeat the work. Those choices help an explanation reach someone in a different place or language. A learner can return to the headword, the position of a pan or the power of the unknown and check the step where they became uncertain. The written detail supports that return.

We can now return to our opening question and ask what Islamic civilization contributed to these journeys.


What did Islam give to knowledge?

The same kinds of work recur across the talk. In Abbasid Baghdad, translation made Greek medicine available to Arabic readers. In Toledo, Ibn Sina reached Latin readers. People could teach from books whose authors wrote for someone else, in another language.

Ibn al-Haytham told a reader how to arrange an observation of light. When a table in the Almagest was recalculated, people using the Latin version gained another way to check their work against the page. Both cases give the learner something to try.

Al-Khwarizmi gave the learner a diagram that explains why a rule works. Once you can explain the rule yourself, you can show someone else what a new step does. Many of the people who did this work in Islamic societies learned from traditions that had travelled a long way to reach them.

Books from Timbuktu reached later readers because people kept and cared for them. Jawi readers could study a Malay chronicle. Ottoman Turkish readers could navigate a sea atlas. Each work put its inherited script to a different use. Keep one object or page in mind. Who found it, tested it, explained it, or kept it available?

Questions and answers

We can finish by returning to two questions.

How did the numerals reach Europe?

We can trace more than one route. The decimal system developed in India, and Arabic writers learned and explained it. In a Latin manuscript, a form of al-Khwarizmi’s name stands at the beginning of an explanation of reckoning. Fibonacci learnt the notation in a Mediterranean setting and explained it for readers in Latin. A person might have met the numbers in a translated book, in a merchant’s workplace, or through someone teaching that practice. The forms did not become standard everywhere at the same speed. In a Renaissance printed arithmetic book you can see what teaching with those signs looked like in a later setting. The separate witnesses let us ask where people learned to calculate and whose methods they had access to.

What did the pinhole experiment show?

It shows a particular effect. Light from the top of an object passes through the opening to a lower position on the screen. Light from the bottom reaches a higher position. Moving the screen changes the size of that image in a predictable way. The photograph and the model help us check different things. The photograph shows that an image actually formed. The model keeps the geometry simple enough that we can trace each path. A new observer can repeat the arrangement and compare what they see with what was predicted. Kepler later applied optical geometry to a different question, the image on the retina.
